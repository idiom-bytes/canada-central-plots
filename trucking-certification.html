Canada’s Heavy-Duty Truck Certification Gap

A U.S. agency restructuring shifted which body certifies heavy-duty truck emissions. Canada’s import regulations haven’t been updated to reflect it. The practical result: Canadian dealers may not be able to order 2027-model trucks unless the gap is closed before August 2026.

Sectors Affected: 6 Trucks/Year: ~20,000 Ordering Window: Aug 2026 Fix Required: 1 regulation amendment
1
Context

Canada’s heavy-duty fleet is sourced from the U.S.

Canada does not manufacture its own heavy-duty commercial trucks. American manufacturers — Kenworth, Peterbilt, Freightliner, International, Mack — supply 95% of Canada’s heavy-duty truck fleet. This long-standing trade relationship has worked smoothly because Canadian and U.S. emissions and safety standards have been closely aligned.

The Regulatory Gap

A U.S. agency restructuring left a gap in Canadian import regs

Canada’s import regulations for heavy-duty trucks recognize emissions certifications issued by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). This has been the standard arrangement for decades.

The U.S. subsequently restructured responsibility for heavy-duty vehicle certifications, moving them to the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA). Canadian regulations cite the EPA as the recognized certifying body and have not yet been updated to reflect this change. As a result, new trucks certified by NHTSA cannot currently be imported under Canadian rules.

Timeline

The ordering window arrives in August 2026

The gap was formally created on February 12, 2026, when the U.S. EPA rescinded its authority over GHG vehicle certifications. But the trajectory was clear from March 2025, when the Trump administration announced its intent — leaving over a year between the announcement and the August 2026 ordering window.

May 2018
Canada harmonizes HD GHG regulations with U.S. EPA Phase 2. Certification language: “any engine certified by EPA may be sold in Canada.”
April 2024
Biden EPA finalizes Phase 3 GHG standards for MY2027+, as an EPA-only rule. Canada does not update its regulations to reflect the new standard.
March 2025
Trump EPA announces intent to rescind the 2009 Endangerment Finding — the legal basis for all GHG vehicle certifications. U.S. manufacturers immediately flag Ottawa: EPA certification will cease to exist for 2027+ trucks.
February 12, 2026
Trump EPA formally rescinds all GHG vehicle emission standards. EPA no longer has authority to certify heavy-duty trucks. NHTSA becomes the only remaining federal certifier. The gap is now a legal fact.
May 2026
Canadian Truck Dealers Association raises the issue publicly. Government has acknowledged the gap and indicated it is working toward a resolution.
August 2026 — Ordering Window
Canadian fleets make annual renewal decisions for the 2027 model year. Regulatory alignment needs to be in place before this window.

Financial Impact

A $4–5B direct disruption across the supply chain

Class 8 trucks are almost entirely built-to-order — there is no dealer floor stock for semis. Missing the ordering window means missing the supply for that model year entirely. With ~17,000–20,000 Class 8 trucks sold in Canada annually at an average of ~$265,000 CAD each, the direct purchase market at risk is $4.5–5.3B CAD. That is the first-order number. The broader supply chain impact compounds from there.

Note on confidence

The $4–5B direct figure is well-grounded in unit volumes and pricing data. The downstream multiplier ($3.25–6.5B rate pressure) is an order-of-magnitude estimate. No analyst has modeled this as a standalone scenario because until recently it was considered an unlikely outcome. The uncertainty band is wide; downstream figures should be treated as order-of-magnitude estimates.

6
In Plain Terms

A standards gap on paper, not in practice

Both countries share aligned safety and emissions outcomes. There is no technical disagreement. The issue is that Canadian import regulations reference the EPA as the certifying body, and that reference has not been updated since NHTSA assumed that role in the U.S.

Canada’s Motor Vehicle Safety Act regulations cite the EPA as the recognized certifying body. NHTSA now issues those certifications. Updating the citation resolves the issue.

The gap, in plain terms

Canada’s regulations cite EPA as the certifying body.
U.S. certifications are now issued by NHTSA.
Amending the regulation to recognize NHTSA closes the gap.

The technical standards themselves do not need to change.

↓
Recommendation

Three steps to resolve this before August

📝

Amend the Motor Vehicle Safety Act regulations

Transport Canada should amend the regulations to recognize NHTSA certifications as equivalent to EPA certifications for heavy-duty truck imports. The technical standards are already aligned — this is an administrative citation update, not a policy debate. When the UK’s vehicle type approval regulations were left citing a body that no longer had authority after Brexit, targeted statutory instruments (SI 2019/648) updated the certifying body references — the same mechanism available under Canada’s MVSA.
Feasibility — High
Administrative citation update via statutory instrument — no new policy required
Impact — Direct
Closes the import gap immediately upon amendment
📅

Deliver a formal amendment before August 2026

Fleet renewal ordering decisions happen in August. A ministerial interim order may be the fastest first step, but it expires in 12 months and is not a durable fix. A full regulatory amendment with a public completion date is the correct outcome.
Feasibility — Medium
Requires departmental prioritization within a tight window
Impact — Critical
Saves the 2027 model year order cycle for Canadian fleets
🔄

Adopt ambulatory references in MVSA regulations

Transport Canada’s own January–March 2026 MVSA consultation explicitly raised whether references to foreign certifying bodies should be “ambulatory” — automatically tracking changes — rather than fixed to specific versions. The current gap is a direct consequence of a fixed EPA citation. Making MVSA references ambulatory would prevent future certification gaps structurally, without requiring emergency legislation each time a U.S. agency restructures.
Feasibility — Medium
Transport Canada raised this in its 2026 MVSA consultation — the policy groundwork exists
Impact — Preventive
Eliminates this class of problem: future certifying body restructurings would not create import gaps
