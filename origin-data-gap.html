Methodology & Key Finding

Canada does not record who commits these crimes.

We assembled a decade-long database of Canadian sexual-exploitation, human-trafficking and child-exploitation cases. Then we asked a simple question the official statistics cannot answer: what was the offender's immigration status or country of origin?

— of distinct cases give no indication of the offender's immigration status or country of origin.

Why this gap exists

The data was never collected, by design

Statistics Canada's Uniform Crime Reporting Survey — the national record of police-reported crime — does not capture the accused's citizenship, immigration status, or country of birth. Neither courts nor the Correctional Service publish offender nationality in any routine, aggregated form. There is no Canadian equivalent of the offender-origin breakdowns that, after years of public pressure, were eventually published in the United Kingdom's grooming-gang reviews.

That leaves news coverage as the only public signal of who is involved. So we measured it directly across every case in our database. The result: coverage almost never states it either.

This is the finding, not a side note. The absence of origin data means no one — neither those who suspect a link to immigration nor those who deny it — can measure it. The question is rendered unanswerable by the structure of the data itself. A democracy cannot debate what it refuses to count.
The UK precedent. Britain spent years insisting offender ethnicity in grooming-gang cases was not recorded or not relevant. When the data was finally compelled into the open, it reshaped the national debate. Canada is at the stage the UK was a decade ago: the data is simply not kept.

A note on interpretation: these figures measure what is reported and recorded, not the share of crime attributable to any group. They show the measurement gap — not its direction.

Why Canada stopped counting — the cultural background

Background, not argument. The gap above is a measurement fact; the reason behind it is historical. For roughly three decades Canada deliberately did not collect crime data disaggregated by race or origin — a settled policy, debated and re-affirmed, not an oversight.

  • 1945

    Framing. Statistics Canada generally uses “visible minority” and multiculturalism categories rather than the language of “race.”

  • 1988

    Canadian Multiculturalism Act enacted — multiculturalism becomes official policy and national self-image.

  • 1990

    The plan, and its reversal. The Canadian Centre for Justice Statistics announces it will record the race of accused and victims through the UCR. After public debate — and with some police services declining — the Chief Statistician withdraws it within weeks.

  • 1992
    –2020
    ~30 years of silenceNo national crime data disaggregated by race or origin — collected or published
  • 2017

    Ontario Anti-Racism Act — enables race-based data collection in public services to identify systemic racism.

  • 2020

    UCR initiative begins. StatCan and the Chiefs of Police commit to collecting Indigenous and racialized identity data (“PIRID”).

  • 2024

    UCR updated to collect Indigenous and racialized identity; pilot stage, national data not expected before ~2027.

The culture beneath it: the original opposition came largely from racialized communities, who feared the figures would be used against them — so the silence began as a protective instinct. Since 2020 the logic has reversed: advocates now argue that without data, systemic disparities cannot be addressed. The same value has been read both ways.

The distinction — and how other democracies report it

One distinction is easy to miss. “Race” and “country of origin / immigration status” are two different questions and reporting either, in aggregate, has never been an issue in peer democracies. The only reason Canada cannot answer the origin question is that it has chosen not to record it.

Race & Indigenous identity

What Canada's reform now measures. Reintroduced via the UCR since 2017 (pilot; ~2027). With the purpose of detecting systemic racism.

Immigration status · Country of birth · Nationality

What this page is about. Not collected, not part of the current initiative — no field, no publication, no plan.

Race and origin are not interchangeable. A citizen and a recent arrival may share a racialized identity; two immigrants from different countries may be recorded identically on race. So even a fully successful race-data program leaves the origin question exactly where it is now: unmeasured.

The solution already exists - Canada could adopt it

Aggregate reporting of offending by national origin or immigrant background is a normal official statistic across Western Europe, each with population denominators and explicit caveats.

Recommendations

None of the following is technically difficult or unprecedented. Each is already done by Canada in another domain, or by peer democracies in this one. They remain unimplemented as a matter of choice.

  • Add country-of-birth and immigration-status fields to the UCR

    The same survey now being expanded to record race could carry these variables in the same update.

  • Publish offender origin in aggregate, with denominators

    Canada already publishes population by religion, ethnic origin and place of birth — down to the neighbourhood — through the Census.

  • Link justice records to immigration records

    StatCan already runs the Longitudinal Immigration Database (IMDB), linking landings to tax data. A justice × immigration linkage is the same technique.

  • Publish a recurring “crime by origin” statistic

    Exactly what Germany, Denmark, Norway, the Netherlands and Sweden already release on a schedule.

  • Do it to a rigorous, non-inflammatory standard

    Rates with denominators, age- and sex-standardised, aggregate-only with small-cell suppression — never a claim about any group.

The problem isn't a high or low number. The problem is that no one can measure it. A democracy that publishes the religion of the family next door, but not the immigration status of a convicted trafficker, has made a choice about what it is willing to know.
