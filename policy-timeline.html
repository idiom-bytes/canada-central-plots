How the Channel Was Built

The half-million asylum claimants did not arrive by accident, and not by force — the land border to the south is the most heavily policed on earth. The growth came through the front door, by policy: a chain of discrete federal decisions, each with a date, an instrument, and a minister. This view lays them out in order. The striking pattern: the decisions that opened the channel were almost all regulations and ministerial instructions — executive acts that never went to a vote — while Parliament's actual bills mostly arrived later, to slow it down.

What the timeline shows

The back door was left open. None of this would matter as much if a refused claim led to departure. It largely doesn't: the Auditor General (tabled 8 Jul 2020) found CBSA did not know the whereabouts of ~34,700 people under removal orders — two-thirds of the cases it sampled — with failed asylum claimants the largest group ordered to leave, most not removed within the one-year target. As of December 2025, ~74,000 failed claimants sat in CBSA's removals inventory. An inland claim is low-risk precisely because the exit rarely follows.

What it costs, and how it really compares. The viral "$5 billion" figure is not a single official number; the documented PBO/IRB figures are specific and smaller: asylum healthcare (IFHP) cost $722M in 2024-25 (PBO projects ~$1B/yr by ~2030), clearing the recent backlog ~$0.5B, and the IRB backlog stands at ~300,000 claims with ~19-month average waits. The contrast with the United States is not that Canada is overrun at the border — it is that Canada keeps admitting and not resolving: as the U.S. tightened, its asylum grant rate fell from 51% to 19% (Feb 2024–Aug 2025), while Canada's IRB inventory quadrupled from 70,223 (end 2022) to 272,440 (end 2024), with waits reaching ~3.7 years. Cross-country figures are indicative only — different systems, definitions and reporting periods.
