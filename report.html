The report

An objective, source-based data investigation · Updated June 2026

Could It Happen Here?

Britain spent two decades uncovering organized, group-based child sexual exploitation — the “grooming gang” scandals of Rotherham, Telford and beyond — while institutions looked away and failed even to record who was responsible. This investigation asks a careful question: is a similar pattern emerging in Canada, and would we even know? It builds a holistic picture — the crime trends, how the problem manifests here, the policies that reshaped screening and enforcement, the courts and removal system, and the data Canada keeps — measured against the UK. It blames no community; the subject is institutions, decisions, and evidence.

Before you read

How this investigation is built, and what it does not claim

This is a review, not an argument. The subject — organized, group-based child sexual exploitation, which Britain came to call “grooming gangs” — is grave and easily sensationalized, so the framing here is deliberately careful and the term is used descriptively, never as a claim about any group. It is written to be read by people across the political spectrum and evaluated on its sources. The standards we hold ourselves to:

  • Primary, official sources for every headline — Statistics Canada, IRCC, the Parliamentary Budget Officer, the Immigration and Refugee Board, the Auditor General, CBSA, the Canada Gazette, and Parliament.
  • Rates, not raw counts — with the denominator disclosed. Population-normalized figures are used wherever a comparison is made.
  • We correct exaggerations even when they suit the thesis — for example, eye-popping “since-inception” crime increases are replaced with honest rates and ten-year changes.
  • We state what the data cannot establish. Canada does not record the immigration status or origin of criminal offenders, so this report does not claim a link between immigration and crime. It reports two things side by side and is explicit that correlation is not causation.
  • The subject is decisions and institutions, not migrants. Accountability here attaches to policy choices and the bodies that made them — never to people by origin or faith. We treat the UK as a precedent and a warning, not a template we assume Canada matches.

Chapter 1: The blind spot - Canada doesn't record it.

Methodology & key finding

99.4% of 3,342 catalogued exploitation & trafficking cases give no indication of the offender's immigration status or country of origin.

Statistics Canada's national crime survey contains no such field, it is unmeasurable by design.

Here the report reaches its most important limit — so we lead with it. Of 3,342 distinct Canadian exploitation and trafficking cases assembled from a decade of coverage, 99.4% give no indication of the offender's immigration status or nationality — because Statistics Canada's crime survey contains no such field. Note the asymmetry: Canada does publish the country of citizenship of asylum claimants, monthly, but records nothing about the origin of those charged with these crimes. The link many assume, in either direction, is unmeasurable by design — and that is the finding, not a workaround for one. Everything that follows should be read in that light.

Chapter 2 · Official data

The problem at home: is it getting worse?

Source note. Chapter 1 measured news coverage and what it omits. This chapter uses official Statistics Canada data — police-reported incidents (UCR, Table 35-10-0177). A different source for a different question: not “who,” but “how much, and is it rising?”

In 2024, Canadian police reported 503 human-trafficking incidents (up +102% since 2016) and 13,108 child-exploitation incidents (up +79%). Every tracked offence rose over the decade; the chart indexes each to 2014 = 100 to show relative growth honestly. These are widely regarded as undercounts — most sexual offences go unreported — and StatCan cautions that part of the rise reflects greater reporting and detection, not occurrence alone.

Police-reported rate per 100k, indexed to 2014=100 (StatCan UCR 35-10-0177).

Per capita, England records roughly 3× Canada's rate of child sexual offences, and UK modern-slavery referrals have risen far faster than Canadian trafficking incidents — but the measures differ and are not directly comparable (see Chapter 7).

Chapter 3

The question, and why it's hard to answer

Britain's grooming-gang scandals exposed organized child sexual exploitation on a scale its institutions had refused to see — and only began to measure after a national reckoning. This investigation asks whether Canada shows early signs of the same problem, and finds the honest answer is partly obscured by Canada's own choices about what to record. What follows is the holistic picture: the conditions, the crime trends, the recording gap, the enforcement response, and the UK precedent.

A theme recurs throughout: a consistent gap between the reassuring official message and the harder number underneath it — starting with the claim that immigration is simply “coming down.”

Chapter 4

The intake: permanent down, temporary still enormous, asylum rising

Canada admits two very different streams: permanent immigrants, who are selected and tracked, and a far larger, faster-moving population of temporary residents — foreign workers, international students and asylum claimants. The temporary population reached 2.56 million and roughly doubled in under four years. As the government capped permits, one component kept rising: asylum.

Temporary-resident population by type (StatCan 17-10-0121). Work and study turn down from their peaks; asylum keeps climbing.

Chapter 5

How the system was built — mostly without a vote

The growth was not an accident at the border; it was assembled by policy. Tracing the decisions that reshaped screening, asylum and enforcement from 2012 onward, a striking pattern emerges: most were executive instruments — Orders in Council, ministerial instructions and a treaty protocol — that never went to a parliamentary vote. The accountability for them lives in the Canada Gazette, not in Hansard. This report tracks 42 such decisions, each with a primary-source citation.

Chapter 6

A justice and enforcement system that can't keep up

Remand (held without conviction) as a share of provincial custody (StatCan 35-10-0154).

Whatever enters the system has to be processed, and the system is overwhelmed. More than three in four people in Canada's provincial jails have not been convicted of anything — they are held on remand, awaiting trials the courts can't deliver in time; the un-convicted share rose from 22.5% in 1978 to 76.6% today. At the enforcement end, removals reached a record ~23,000 in 2025 but were still outpaced by new removable cases, while the asylum backlog sits near 300,000. Effort is up; the gap keeps widening.

Chapter 7

The cautionary template: Canada vs. the UK

Britain spent two decades denying, then uncovering, group-based child sexual exploitation — and only after a national reckoning began recording who was responsible (Baroness Casey's 2025 audit found ethnicity unrecorded in two-thirds of cases; where recorded, disproportionately British-Pakistani). Canada shows the same crimes rising, but cannot run that comparison, because it records no offender origin. On the UK's timeline, Canada sits at roughly its pre-inquiry, c.2010–2014 stage — with the UK's experience available as a warning it has not yet had to learn.

We do not project the UK's offender findings onto Canada. The point is the opposite: Canada has made the question unanswerable here, while the same risk surface grows.

Chapter 8

Who lobbies for the volume

Registered lobbying contacts on immigration per year (Registry of Lobbyists).

Influence over immigration policy is a matter of public record. Across 2008–2024 organizations filed 6,094 registered lobbying contacts on immigration — and the heaviest are not advocacy groups of any faith or ethnicity. They are economic interests: universities, the Business Council of Canada, colleges, student associations, Restaurants Canada and the CFIB — constituencies that benefit from a larger labour pool and tuition-paying international students.

Chapter 9 · Synthesis

Did the policy changes weaken the system?

This is where the report's threads meet. Below, every dated decision is placed on one timeline, coloured by whether it weakened or strengthened the system's capacity to screen, detain, prosecute and remove.

Weakened Strengthened Mixed Unassessed

The timing lines up in one direction: the system-weakening decisions cluster in 2016–2022, while the corrective brakes (bail reform, the study cap, the Borders Act) arrive only in 2023–2026 — and every tracked crime rose over the period in between.

What this establishes is narrower than it may look, and we hold the line on it: the system was being loosened during the years these crimes climbed, and the corrections came only afterward. It does not prove the decisions caused the crime trends — reporting practices, detection and many other factors move these rates — and the brakes are too recent for their effect to show in data ending in 2024.

Chapter 10 · History & culture (background)

Why Canada stopped counting — and why that needn't bind the future

The fix in Chapter 2 is simple; the reason Canada has not taken it is historical. For roughly three decades Canada deliberately did not collect crime data disaggregated by race or origin — a 1990 plan to record it was withdrawn within weeks, and the silence held until the late 2010s. The story is rooted in a particular reading of multiculturalism and, at first, in protective concern from minority communities themselves.

That history is worth understanding — but it is background, not an argument. The methodology now exists and is used by peer democracies, so the cultural and political weight of the past need not decide the future. We present it as context only, and keep it separate from the forward-looking step in Chapter 2.

Chapter 11 · Recommendations

Simple transparency Canada has chosen not to implement

None of the following is technically difficult or unprecedented. Each is already done — either by Canada in another domain, or by peer democracies in this one. They remain unimplemented as a matter of choice.

  • Add country-of-birth and immigration-status fields to the UCR

    The very survey now being expanded to record race could carry these variables in the same update.

  • Publish offender origin in aggregate, with denominators

    Canada already publishes population by religion, ethnic origin and place of birth — down to the neighbourhood — through the Census. The sensitivity bar is already cleared on the population side.

  • Link justice records to immigration records

    Statistics Canada already runs the Longitudinal Immigration Database (IMDB), linking immigrant landings to tax data. A justice × immigration linkage is the same technique, not a new capability.

  • Publish a recurring “crime by origin” statistic

    Exactly what Germany, Denmark, Norway, the Netherlands and Sweden already release on a regular schedule.

  • Do it to a rigorous, non-inflammatory standard

    Rates with denominators, age- and sex-standardised, aggregate-only with small-cell suppression, confounders disclosed — and never framed as a claim about any group.

The point is not that any particular number would be high or low — no one can say, which is precisely the problem. It is that a democracy that publishes the religion of the family next door, but not the immigration status of a convicted trafficker, has made a choice about what it is willing to know.
