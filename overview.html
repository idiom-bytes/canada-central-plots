Canada's Immigration Reality

One question runs through everything here: does what we're told match what the data shows? The government says immigration is coming down. By the numbers, one stream is doing the opposite — and the system driving it was built deliberately, by policy, over a decade. This page is the spine: the claims, the evidence, and the risks now in place. Every section links to the underlying data.

Chapter 1 · The Volume Reality

The claim: “Immigration is coming down.”

True for the front door. Not for asylum.

Permanent residents, study permits and work permits are all falling from their 2024–25 peaks — so the headline is technically true. But the asylum stream is the exception: new claims are up 608% since 2015, and the standing claimant population sits at ~525,000. Net non-permanent residents peaked at +781,000 in a single year (2023/24) — more than every permanent immigrant admitted that year. And the 2.56M temporary residents already inside the country cannot be un-admitted; many can still claim asylum as their status lapses.

Verdict: the headline is true and substantively misleading. The lever the government doesn't fully control is the one that's exploding.

Chapter 2 · The Policy Design

The claim: “It's a border crisis — this happened to us.”

It was built, by policy, on purpose.

The land border to the south is the most heavily policed on earth; the growth came through the front door. We traced 11 federal decisions (2012–2026) that built the channel — the Mexico visa lift, the loosened temporary-worker rules, uncapped international students, the Safe Third Country redirect that pushed claims inland (31% of claims in 2022 → 71% in 2025), and the late-arriving brakes. The striking pattern: 8 of the 11 were executive instruments — Orders in Council, ministerial instructions, an international protocol — that never went to a vote. The actual Acts of Parliament mostly arrived at the bookends, to tighten.

Verdict: not an accident and not the border. A chain of internal decisions, with dates and ministers — accountability that lives in the Gazette, not in Hansard.

Chapter 3 · The Consequences & Risks

The pattern to watch: UK-style intake without integration or enforcement.

What the system produces downstream.

The United Kingdom is the cautionary template — rapid intake plus weak integration plus slow enforcement, producing social-cohesion breakdown, grooming-gang scandals, a surge in antisemitism, and fiscal strain. Chapter 4 · What's Still Broken

The brakes came late and don't reach the structural gaps.

The problems still in place today.

The 2024–26 brakes (study cap, Mexico visa reimposed, Bill C-12) slowed new intake. They did not fix the load-bearing problems: removal rarely follows a refused claim (CBSA lost track of 34,700 under removal orders; ~74,000 failed claimants in inventory), the IRB can't keep pace (~300k backlog, ~19-month waits), the inland pool is already inside, origin is tracked only selectively, and the levers remain executive — reopenable without a vote.

How we keep this objective

This is a review, not a polemic. The standards we hold ourselves to:

  • Primary, official sources for every headline (Statistics Canada, PBO, IRB, Auditor General, IRCC, ESDC, CBSA, the Canada Gazette, Parliament).
  • Rates, not raw counts — and we always disclose the population denominator we used.
  • We correct exaggerations even when they favour the argument (e.g. the viral “$5B” asylum-cost figure is replaced with itemized PBO numbers).
  • We flag contested attributions (e.g. Century Initiative influence — asserted by critics, denied by the minister).
  • We state what the data cannot establish (inland claimants' prior status; offender origin; causation vs. correlation).
  • The subject is decisions and decision-makers, not migrants as individuals.
