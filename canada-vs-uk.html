The Same Experiment, One Step Behind

Britain spent two decades denying, then uncovering, an epidemic of group-based child sexual exploitation — and only after a national reckoning began recording who was responsible. Canada is running a similar experiment in rapid intake and weak enforcement, with the same crimes rising sharply — but it cannot yet see the pattern, because it does not collect the data the UK was finally forced to confront. This is what we can compare, and what we deliberately cannot.

What we can and cannot claim (read this)

  • We are NOT claiming Canada's offenders mirror the UK's. The UK's disproportionate-perpetrator findings come from data Canada does not collect. We cannot run that comparison here — and that absence, not any assumed answer, is the point. See the Origin Data Gap.
  • Cross-country numbers are indicative, not identical. The UK's NRM (all modern slavery) and Canada's UCR “trafficking in persons” are different instruments; UK group-CSE definitions differ from Canada's child-sexual-violation categories. We compare direction and institutional response, not like-for-like counts.
  • Police-reported is an undercount in both countries; reporting and recording practices differ and change over time. Trafficking and child-exploitation offences were also codified relatively recently in Canada, so we report 10-year change, not since-inception (which would be a meaningless base-effect figure).
  • The subject is institutions and decisions, not migrants or any community. The UK lesson is about a system that would not look; the Canadian risk is a system that does not record.
