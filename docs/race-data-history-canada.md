# Disaggregated crime data in Canada: recent policy, with historical background

A neutral, factual summary. It describes events, official measures, and the stated
positions of the parties involved, and quotes primary and official sources directly. It
does not argue for or against any position. The emphasis is on **recent policy
(2017–present)**; earlier history is included as background.

---

## Recent policy (2017–present)

Since the late 2010s, Canadian governments and agencies have moved toward collecting
criminal-justice data disaggregated by Indigenous and racialized identity. Key measures:

- **2017 — Ontario, *Anti-Racism Act*.** Provincial legislation enabling race-based data
  collection in public services. Per a Maytree summary: *"In response to calls for
  action, the Ontario government established the Anti-Racism Act, 2017 and associated
  regulations to require key criminal justice areas to collect race-based data for the
  purpose of identifying and addressing systemic racism and advance racial equity."*
- **Federal commitment.** Per the same summary: *"At the federal level, the Canadian
  government announced its commitment to improve and promote disaggregated race-based
  data as a necessary component to addressing systemic racism in all federal
  institutions."*
- **2020 onward — UCR initiative.** Statistics Canada and the Canadian Association of
  Chiefs of Police began developing the collection of Indigenous and racialized identity
  data through the Uniform Crime Reporting (UCR) Survey ("PIRID" / UCR 2.5).
- **February 2024.** The UCR Survey was updated to enable collection of the Indigenous
  and racialized identity of victims and accused persons.
- **Summer 2025.** Statistics Canada released foundational documents (a "What We Heard"
  report, an analytical framework, and operational guidelines). The program remains in a
  pilot/implementation phase.

**Stated purpose and status (Statistics Canada, June 2025 progress update):**
- *"The collection of data on the Indigenous and racialized identity of all accused and
  victims of crime may prove critical in understanding and addressing disparities in the
  criminal justice system."*
- *"Although Statistics Canada's UCR survey is now system-ready to receive PIRID,
  adoption of UCR 2.5 by police services is voluntary, and changes to their Records
  Management Systems are also required to enable data collection. Given the time required
  for onboarding, national-level data are not expected to be received until at least
  2027."*

**Context cited for the shift (Department of Justice Canada):**
- *"Black people are overrepresented in Canada's criminal justice system as both victims
  and people accused or convicted of crime."*
- *"National disaggregated data on the racialized identity of those who come in contact
  with the criminal justice system remain fairly limited and underreported."*
- *"Canadian criminal courts do not currently collect information on the racialized
  identity of accused people."*

**Rationale stated by data-collection proponents (Ontario Human Rights Commission
materials):**
- *"At the end of the day, when we get this right, what we'll be able to do is identify
  and monitor potential systemic racism."* (Doucette 2019)
- *"It will be argued that the improved collection of race-based data is required to
  evaluate the impact of anti-bias initiatives."*

## Historical background (how the situation arose)

- **Post-1945.** Statistics Canada generally used "visible minority" and multiculturalism
  categories rather than the language of "race." A Maytree summary characterizes this as:
  *"By adopting the language of visible minority and multiculturalism as official policy,
  it minimized the legacy and impacts of systemic racism and colonialism on the
  well-being of Indigenous, Black, and racialized communities."*
- **1988.** The *Canadian Multiculturalism Act* was enacted.
- **1990.** The Canadian Centre for Justice Statistics announced a plan to collect the
  race of accused persons and victims through the UCR. After public debate — and with
  some police services (including Toronto and Ottawa) declining to participate — the
  Chief Statistician withdrew the plan within weeks, citing sensitivity and limited
  police cooperation.
- **~1992–2020.** Race- and origin-disaggregated crime data were, in practice, not
  collected or published at the national level.

Reasons cited at the time for not collecting the data included: concern (including from
some racialized communities) that figures could be used to justify discrimination;
the multiculturalism framing above; concerns about the reliability of officer-perceived
race and about confounding factors; and limited police cooperation.

---

## Scope note: race vs. immigration origin

The Canadian measures above concern **race and Indigenous identity**. They do **not**
cover **immigration status, country of birth, or nationality**. Crime statistics
disaggregated by immigration origin are not part of Canada's national collection and are
not part of the current initiative. Several other countries — e.g. Germany, Denmark,
Norway, the Netherlands, Sweden — do publish crime data disaggregated by national origin
or immigrant background (see [source-links.md](source-links.md)).
