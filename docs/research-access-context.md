# Research: Public Access, Fee-Simple Status, and Reserve-Land Context

Companion research file for `docs/kb-bc-treaty-land.md`. This addresses the
unresolved research questions in section 2 of that file relating to the
tweet's claim that "some treaty lands could also have limited or NO public
access." Findings below are drawn from primary legal-agreement text
(quoted directly from official BC government / BC Laws / Nisga'a Lisims
sources where possible), BC Treaty Commission materials, and news coverage
from the same Aug 10–12, 2026 news cycle that produced the original tweet.
All URLs are full and directly checkable.

**Bottom line for the report:** the "no public access" claim is not
baseless invention, but it is also not the norm. The documented, factual
picture from BC's five already-effective modern treaties is: (1) settlement
land becomes ordinary fee-simple private land, subject to the same public
access rules as any other private land in BC — meaning the *default* is the
same as private land generally (owner can restrict access, same as any
other landowner); (2) every one of the five treaties examined contains
*explicit public-access-preserving clauses* going beyond what an ordinary
private landowner would be required to offer (recreational access
guarantees, protected-area carve-outs, cooperative park management); and
(3) there are also real, named, treaty-text-documented instances of access
being narrowed — a whole island designated "private lands" with restricted
access (Tla'amin/Ahgykson), and an explicit clause letting the Nisga'a
Nation close certain roads to the public. So the honest framing is: **public
access on treaty settlement land is generally preserved by explicit treaty
provisions, but the treaties also plainly contemplate and permit narrower
access in defined circumstances — this is a negotiated, documented balance,
not a blank check for closure, and not a guarantee of blanket openness
either.**

## 1. Public access provisions under BC's five already-effective modern treaties

### 1a. Nisga'a Final Agreement (effective May 11, 2000)

**Land status:** Nisga'a Lands are owned by the Nisga'a Nation in fee
simple — "the largest estate known in law" — not subject to Land Act
conditions. Source: Nisga'a Final Agreement, Chapter 3 (Lands),
https://www2.gov.bc.ca/assets/gov/environment/natural-resource-stewardship/consulting-with-first-nations/agreements/chapter_03-lands.pdf

**Access chapter (Chapter 6 — Access), primary text via BC Legislative
Library:**
https://www.llbc.leg.bc.ca/public/pubdocs/bcdocs2011/322768/ACCESS.HTML
- Para 2: "Nisga'a Lisims Government will allow reasonable public access to
  and onto Nisga'a Public Lands for temporary non-commercial and
  recreational uses," excluding resource harvesting/extraction, damage, or
  interference with other authorized uses.
- Para 13: if a Nisga'a Village Lands or Nisga'a Private Lands designation
  would block access to an area with a pre-existing public right of access
  (e.g., navigable waters, Crown roads), "Nisga'a Lisims Government will
  provide reasonable alternative means of public access."

**Roads and Rights of Way chapter (Chapter 7), primary text:**
https://www.llbc.leg.bc.ca/public/pubdocs/bcdocs2011/322768/ROADSANDRIGHTSOFWAY.HTML
- Para 60(1): **"the Nisga'a Nation may close to the public any portion of
  a Nisga'a road that is not within Nisga'a Village Lands."** This is a
  real, documented, currently-in-force treaty clause granting a First
  Nation government the legal authority to close certain roads to public
  access — direct precedent for the general shape of the tweet's claim,
  though narrow in scope (non-village Nisga'a roads only).
- Para 60(3): Nisga'a Villages must permit public use of roads on Village
  Lands "that would be open to the public in comparable communities
  elsewhere in British Columbia," and may only close such roads "for
  safety reasons."
- The Nisga'a Highway itself remains BC-owned Crown land, used as a public
  highway, unaffected by the treaty.

**Assessment:** Nisga'a Lands is the one case among the five where the
research turned up an explicit, named treaty clause authorizing a First
Nation government to close roads to the public outright (not just
"private-land-style" default rules) — this is the strongest documented
precedent for the tweet's claim, but it applies specifically to certain
Nisga'a roads outside village lands, not to the land generally, and is
paired with a public-access guarantee provision (Chapter 6) covering
Nisga'a Public Lands.

### 1b. Tsawwassen First Nation Final Agreement (effective April 3, 2009)

**Land status:** 724 ha total settlement land (372 ha former provincial
Crown land + ~290 ha former Indian reserve), owned in fee simple, not
subject to BC Land Act conditions. First treaty to bring a First Nation
fully out of the Indian Act reserve system into a fee-simple,
self-governing land base. Sources: Tsawwassen First Nation Final Agreement
Act, https://www.bclaws.gov.bc.ca/civix/document/id/complete/statreg/07039_01
; Tsawwassen Lands fact sheet,
https://www2.gov.bc.ca/assets/gov/zzzz-to-be-moved/9efbd86da302a0712e6559bdb2c7f9dd/9efbd86da302a0712e6559bdb2c7f9dd/agreements/factsheet_final_tsawwassen_lands.pdf

**Access:** The Final Agreement's standard-form interest documents
(leases, licences, etc.) on Tsawwassen Lands include public
right-of-way provisions as a condition of the interest — i.e., access
rights-of-way were written into the settlement package rather than left
to case-by-case negotiation. No news reporting was found of any documented
instance of public access being closed or restricted on Tsawwassen
settlement land since 2009.

**Documented land-use change (not an access restriction, but the closest
factual analogue to the "condos" rumour in the source tweet):**
Tsawwassen Mills, a 1.2 million sq ft shopping mall, was built on
Tsawwassen treaty settlement land (Class 1 agricultural land near the
Fraser River delta) and opened October 5, 2016, after the land was removed
from BC's Agricultural Land Reserve as part of the treaty package. This is
a real, documented case of treaty settlement land being converted from
open agricultural land to a large private commercial development — the
mall itself remains open to the public as a normal commercial property,
but it is offered here as the one verified factual instance of fee-simple
"development potential" of the kind the source tweet's unverified "condos"
rumour speculated about elsewhere. Sources: Wikipedia,
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsawwassen_Mills ; Viewpoint Vancouver,
https://viewpointvancouver.ca/2016/09/02/agricultural-landa-flyway-and-a-mega-mall-why-this-matters/
; BC government ALR fact sheet,
https://www2.gov.bc.ca/assets/gov/zzzz-to-be-moved/9efbd86da302a0712e6559bdb2c7f9dd/9efbd86da302a0712e6559bdb2c7f9dd/agreements/factsheet_aip_tsawwassen_agriculture_land_reserve.pdf

### 1c. Maa-nulth First Nations Final Agreement (effective April 1, 2011)

Covers five Nations (Huu-ay-aht, Ka:'yu:'k't'h'/Che:k'tles7et'h',
Toquaht, Uchucklesaht, Ucluelet) on the west coast of Vancouver Island,
~24,550 ha of fee-simple settlement land. Source: Maa-nulth First Nations
Final Agreement Act, https://www.bclaws.gov.bc.ca/civix/document/id/complete/statreg/07043_08

**Access — direct quote, primary/official fact sheet, "Access" chapter
summary** (via BC government legal text as summarized in the Final
Agreement and repeated in official fact sheets): "each Maa-nulth First
Nation has the same rights and obligations in respect of public access to
its Maa-nulth First Nation Lands as other owners of estates in fee simple
have in respect of public access to their land," and "each Maa-nulth First
Nation's liability for public access ... is comparable to the liability of
the provincial Crown for public access to unoccupied provincial Crown
lands." This is the clearest primary-source statement among the five
treaties that settlement land access defaults to **ordinary private-land
rules** — neither more open nor more closed than any other fee-simple
parcel in BC.

**Parks — verified by direct PDF read of the official BC government fact
sheet**, "Maa-nulth Final Agreement: Parks,"
https://www2.gov.bc.ca/assets/gov/environment/natural-resource-stewardship/consulting-with-first-nations/agreements/fact_sheet_final_maanulth_parks.pdf
- Pacific Rim National Park Reserve: portions within Maa-nulth First Nation
  Areas may become part of Pacific Rim National Park; the federal Minister
  retains overall management authority; a separate Canada–Maa-nulth
  agreement governs cooperative planning/management. Public national-park
  access is not removed.
- Thunderbird's Nest (T'iitsk'in Paawats), ~2,185 ha of culturally
  significant land on Vancouver Island, was **added** to protection: BC
  agreed to remove it from the working (loggable) forest land base and
  protect it as a new provincial protected area under the treaty — an
  access/conservation *gain*, not a loss.
- Maa-nulth First Nations may participate in provincial park/protected
  area public management processes for parks overlapping their treaty
  areas, rather than being excluded from them.

**Assessment:** Maa-nulth is the strongest documented case of public
access being formally equated to ordinary private-land rules, plus a
concrete example (Thunderbird's Nest) of treaty-driven land protection
expanding rather than reducing public/environmental access.

### 1d. Tla'amin Final Agreement (effective April 5, 2016)

~8,322 ha settlement land (6,405 ha former provincial Crown + 1,917 ha
former reserve), fee simple. Verified directly from the official BC
government fact sheet PDF (read in full):
https://www2.gov.bc.ca/assets/gov/environment/natural-resource-stewardship/consulting-with-first-nations/agreements/fact_sheet_tlaamin_lands.pdf

Direct quotes from that fact sheet:
- **Access chapter:** "The Final Agreement includes provisions to ensure
  public access for rights-of-way and navigable waters, and specific
  access for owners of adjacent fee-simple parcels and tenure-holders...
  Highways will remain provincial Crown land and are specifically excluded
  from Tla'amin Nation Lands." Access is also preserved for law
  enforcement, emergency response, and public utilities.
- **Tla'amin Public Lands:** "With the exception of those lands designated
  Tla'amin Private Lands, Tla'amin will allow reasonable public access on
  Tla'amin Lands for temporary recreational and non-commercial purposes,
  such as hunting, fishing, hiking and canoeing." Tla'amin may require
  permits/licences/waivers from non-members.
- **Tla'amin Private Lands — the clearest documented, named example of
  access restriction found in this research:** "Tla'amin may designate
  portions of Tla'amin Lands as Tla'amin Private Lands and restrict public
  access. Prior to making such a designation, Tla'amin will inform and
  consider the interests of Canada, British Columbia and the public.
  **Ahgykson (Harwood Island) will be designated as Tla'amin Private Lands
  on the effective date of the treaty**" — i.e., an entire island (part of
  the settlement package) was, by the treaty's own terms and on its very
  effective date, converted to private land with restricted public access.
  This is a real, sourced, named precedent for "limited or reduced public
  access" — but it is disclosed openly in the treaty text itself, not a
  hidden or unexpected outcome.
- **Sunshine Coast Trail:** "Tla'amin Nation will allow reasonable public
  access to those portions of the Sunshine Coast Trail that are on
  Tla'amin Lands for temporary recreational purposes." Public access to
  provincial parks generally is unaffected by the treaty (per BC Gov News
  release, https://news.gov.bc.ca/releases/2016ARR0027-000516).

**Assessment:** Tla'amin is the treaty that most directly substantiates
the tweet's claim — it contains a plain-language provision letting the
Nation designate land as private and restrict access, and it names a
specific island (Ahgykson/Harwood Island) where that happened immediately
on the treaty's effective date. This should be reported factually. It
should also be reported with its full context: the designation was
disclosed in the treaty text itself (not secret), required the Nation to
"inform and consider the interests" of Canada, BC, and the public first,
and coexists with explicit public-recreation guarantees on the rest of
Tla'amin Lands and continued access to provincial parks/trails.

### 1e. Yale First Nation Final Agreement (effective April 3, 2013)

~1,966 ha settlement land (217 ha former reserve + 1,749 ha Crown land),
fee simple. Source: Yale First Nation Final Agreement Act,
https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/Y-0.2/FullText.html ; BC gov fact
sheet, https://www2.gov.bc.ca/assets/gov/environment/natural-resource-stewardship/consulting-with-first-nations/agreements/fact_sheet_yale_lands.pdf

- "With respect to provincial laws, Yale First Nation Land...will be
  treated as private land." Public access, occupation, and trespass rules
  on Yale Lands become "subject to the same rights, obligations and
  limitations as other private lands" — again, the ordinary-private-land
  default.
- Explicit carve-out: Yale First Nation "will allow reasonable public
  access to Frozen Lakes Land for temporary recreational uses and
  temporary non-commercial purposes, including reasonable opportunities
  for the public to hunt and fish" — a named, guaranteed recreational
  access commitment written directly into the settlement.

## 2. What a BC treaty settlement legally represents

**Fee simple, not a special "no-access zone":** Across all five treaties
above, settlement land is converted to fee-simple ownership — "the largest
estate known in law" (Nisga'a Final Agreement language) — the same legal
category as any privately owned house, farm, or forestry lot in BC. It is
registered in the provincial Land Title Office (per the Tla'amin fact
sheet) and subject to federal and provincial laws generally, alongside the
Nation's own law-making authority over its own land (zoning, planning,
development). It is not Indian Act reserve land, and it is not some novel
category exempt from BC's land title system.

**The exchange (certainty for defined rights):** BC Treaty Commission's
own explanation (bctreaty.ca) frames a treaty as a nation-to-nation,
constitutionally protected agreement that "identif[ies], define[s] and
implement[s] a range of rights and obligations" and provides legal
**certainty** — "uncertainty about how and where [Aboriginal] rights apply
can discourage investment and economic development in BC. Treaties
provide certainty and will clarify aboriginal rights and title, ownership
of BC's land and resources." (https://bctreaty.ca/negotiations/why-treaties/
, https://bctreaty.ca/faq/) Settlement lands are explicitly described as
"only a percentage of their traditional territory" — e.g., the Tla'amin
fact sheet states the ~8,322 ha settlement package is "approximately 2.6
per cent of Tla'amin's traditional territory" (609,000 ha).

**Important nuance — modern treaties do NOT use blanket "extinguishment"
language:** Older Canadian historic treaties extinguished Aboriginal title
outright in exchange for reserves. Post-1990s BC treaties explicitly moved
away from that. BC Treaty Commission FAQ: "extinguishment and surrender of
rights, in form or result, do not have any place in modern-day Crown
Indigenous relations" — treaties are instead "grounded in the recognition,
affirmation and implementation of Aboriginal title and rights" across the
whole traditional territory, with the treaty defining specific,
certain rights within it. The Nisga'a treaty (the first modern BC treaty)
uses a "modification" model: Nisga'a Aboriginal rights, including title,
"are modified, and continue as modified, as set out in that Agreement" —
rather than "cede, release and surrender" language used in historic
treaties. Sources: https://bctreaty.ca/faq/ ; RCAANC "Certainty" page
(archived), https://www.rcaanc-cirnac.gc.ca/eng/1100100031307/1543410336192
; Nisga'a Final Agreement Act text, https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/n-23.3/FullText.html?wbdisable=true

**A caveat the report should flag, because it is directly relevant to "who
really owns fee-simple land" and broke in the same period as this
research:** *Cowichan Tribes v. Canada (Attorney General)*, 2025 BCSC 1490
(decided Aug 7, 2025) — a BC Supreme Court ruling that Aboriginal title can
be found to persist over land **already held in fee simple by private
parties and government**, in a Richmond, BC case involving land that was
**never part of a modern treaty settlement** (the Cowichan never signed a
BC treaty). The Court found Canada's and Richmond's fee-simple titles in
some lots "defective and invalid," suspended for 18 months, and ordered
Crown-Cowichan negotiation; it did not invalidate private landowners'
titles and the Cowichan did not ask it to. BC announced it would appeal.
This is important context but should **not be conflated** with the treaty
settlement land discussed above — it is about the separate, ongoing
question of Aboriginal title on land that was never subject to a treaty at
all, and if anything illustrates that treaty settlement (which converts
land to fee simple with the Nation's own agreement and defined
certainty) is one of the few mechanisms that forecloses this kind of
title uncertainty, rather than creating it. Sources: Cassels Brock legal
bulletin, https://cassels.com/insights/aboriginal-title-supersedes-fee-simple-landmark-ruling-in-cowichan-tribes-v-canada-attorney-general-creates-significant-uncertainty-for-private-landowners-in-bc/
; Mandell Pinder case summary, https://www.mandellpinder.com/cowichan-tribes-v-canada-attorney-general-2025-bcsc-1490-case-summary/
; Wikipedia summary, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cowichan_Tribes_v._Canada

## 3. Reserve land in BC today, as size comparison for the 135,425 ha pending commitment

**Best available aggregate figure found:** The *Encyclopedia of British
Columbia* (KnowBC / Harbour Publishing reference work) states: "There are
198 bands in BC and 1,700 reserves amounting to 4,734.85 sq km or about
.4% of the province's total land area." (https://www.knowbc.com/knowbc/Books/Encyclopedia-of-BC/R/Reserves)
That is **473,485 hectares** — roughly **3.5 times larger** than the
135,425 ha of current Final/Incremental Treaty Agreement commitments
listed in the 2025/26 Public Accounts Note 28. Caveat: this
encyclopedia entry does not itself cite a primary government source or
publication date for the figure, so it should be labeled a secondary
reference figure, not an ISC/StatCan-certified number — no ISC or
StatCan publication with a clean province-wide aggregate hectare total
could be located in this research pass (ISC/CIRNAC publish reserve data
per-band/per-community, e.g. via "First Nation Profiles," rather than as a
single province-wide rollup figure). Related counts corroborated elsewhere:
1,583–1,700 reserves associated with ~198–203 First Nations bands in BC
(figures vary slightly by year/source) — e.g. the Canadian Encyclopedia
article "Reserves in British Columbia,"
https://www.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/article/reserves-in-british-columbia
(cites similar reserve/band counts; a historical note there and in UBC's
Indigenous Foundations project, https://indigenousfoundations.arts.ubc.ca/reserves/
, is that BC reserves were set unusually small compared to the rest of
Canada — "an average of 20 acres granted per family," versus 160–640 acres
per family of five under the Numbered Treaties on the Prairies — which is
directly relevant background for why BC's reserve land base is small
relative to other provinces and why the treaty process exists at all in a
province where, unlike most of Canada, historic treaties were never
signed over most of the land base).

**National context:** Canada-wide, reserve land totals roughly 2.6 million
hectares, about 0.2% of Canada's land mass, across ~3,100+ reserves
(various sources, consistent figure).

**For the report:** frame the 135,425 ha pending commitment as roughly a
third to a half again as large as BC's entire existing reserve land base —
a genuinely large number in absolute terms — while being clear that (a)
it is a Public Accounts *contingent-liability/commitment* figure covering
land not yet transferred in most cases, not a completed transfer, and (b)
existing reserve land in BC (~473,485 ha per the best available secondary
source) is itself under 0.4% of the province's ~92.25 million ha land
area, and treaty settlement land, once transferred, becomes ordinary
fee-simple private land rather than reserve land.

## 4. BC Treaty Commission six-stage process, and Final Agreement vs. Incremental Treaty Agreement

**Six-stage process** (set out in the 1991 BC Claims Task Force Report,
adopted in the 1992 tripartite BC Treaty Commission Agreement). Source:
https://bctreaty.ca/negotiations/negotiation-process/ ; corroborating
clean primary-source language from the official Maa-nulth fact sheet
(read directly from PDF): "The negotiation of a Final Agreement marks
Stage Five of the six-stage British Columbia treaty process, and is the
conclusion of substantive treaty negotiations. Once ratified by all
parties, the Final Agreement will become a treaty through legislation. It
will be a constitutionally-protected, legal agreement which creates
mutually binding obligations and commitments."
(https://www2.gov.bc.ca/assets/gov/environment/natural-resource-stewardship/consulting-with-first-nations/agreements/fact_sheet_final_maanulth_parks.pdf)

1. **Statement of Intent** — First Nation files notice of its governing
   body, mandate, and traditional territory (noting overlaps).
2. **Readiness to Negotiate** — all three parties (First Nation, Canada,
   BC) show they have mandate, negotiators, and ratification procedures
   ready; the Commission declares the table open.
3. **Framework Agreement** — parties agree on the topics to be negotiated
   and a timetable.
4. **Agreement in Principle (AIP)** — substantive negotiation of land,
   governance, financial, and rights provisions.
5. **Finalization → Final Agreement** — legal/technical issues resolved;
   signed and ratified; becomes a treaty via settlement legislation;
   constitutionally protected once in force.
6. **Implementation** — the treaty's terms are phased in over time per the
   agreed implementation plan.

**Final Agreement:** a completed, ratified, legally binding treaty
(Stage 5→6) — a full, comprehensive settlement of land, self-government,
and rights matters. Nisga'a, Tsawwassen, Maa-nulth, Tla'amin, and Yale
are all Final Agreements now in effect. This is the category that fully
"settles" a Nation's treaty relationship.

**Incremental Treaty Agreement (ITA):** a *separate, bilateral* agreement
between the Province and a First Nation still negotiating a treaty — not a
substitute for a Final Agreement. Official BC government description:
"An ITA is a legally binding pre-treaty agreement negotiated by the
Province and First Nation or First Nations at a treaty negotiation table.
An ITA is not a replacement for treaty, but rather an incremental step
that advances treaty-related benefits for the First Nations and the
Province prior to a Final Agreement," transferring land in stages "even if
the First Nation never finalizes a treaty," and building trust/momentum
toward eventual settlement. Source:
https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/environment/natural-resource-stewardship/consulting-with-first-nations/first-nations-negotiations/incremental-treaty-agreements

**Why this matters for Note 28's table:** the table in the 2025/26 Public
Accounts explicitly spans *both* categories — "current commitments of
provincial Crown land for all Final Agreement and Incremental Treaty
Agreement tables." That means the 135,425 ha figure is a mix of (a) land
committed under treaties that are fully negotiated/ratified or nearing
that point, and (b) land already transferred or committed via bilateral
ITAs to Nations that may still be years away from (or may never reach) a
comprehensive Final Agreement. These are legally different stages of
commitment and the report should not treat the combined total as if it
describes one uniform process.

**Adjacent finding relevant to Chapter 1 framing (flagging for the other
chapter, not fully researched here):** three Nations in the Note 28 table
— Kitselas, Kitsumkalum, and K'ómoks — have already **ratified** Final
Agreement treaties in 2025 (Kitselas ratified April 10, 2025; K'ómoks
ratified March 8, 2025 and received Royal Assent; Kitsumkalum ratified
November 1, 2025), with implementation/Effective Date processes underway
or pending. Kitselas and Kitsumkalum alone account for roughly 60% of the
135,425 ha total (35,290 ha + 45,789 ha ≈ 81,079 ha). This means a large
share of the "current commitments" figure is not speculative
still-under-negotiation land but land tied to treaties already ratified
by the affected Nations and the BC Legislature, in the same "already
enacted, awaiting effective date" category the rest of this research
treats as governed by real precedent. Sources: BC Treaty Commission,
https://bctreaty.ca/treaties-and-agreements/modern-treaties/ ; BC Gov
News, https://news.gov.bc.ca/releases/2026PREM0019-000416 ; Newswire
(K'ómoks Royal Assent), https://www.newswire.ca/news-releases/k-omoks-treaty-receives-royal-assent-in-the-bc-legislature-890041452.html

## 5. Directly on point: the current (Aug 2026) Te'mexw/Victoria-area access controversy

This is not a completed/effective treaty (Te'mexw is still in
negotiation), so it falls outside the "already-effective" scope of this
research brief, but it is the live, contemporaneous dispute the source
tweet and its map (kb-bc-treaty-land.md section 1b) are actually about,
and it shows the "public access" question is being actively argued with
citable claims on both sides right now:

- **BC Conservative Party claim** (Aug 11, 2026 statement): "the Te'mexw
  Treaty Association is under no legal obligation to continue to allow
  public access to their newly acquired parks," referring to the pending
  transfer of 30 Crown parcels to Te'mexw including Hatley Castle/Hatley
  Park, Sooke Mountain Park, and Discovery Island Park. This is a
  political party's characterization, not independently verified fact —
  it does not cite treaty text. Source:
  https://conservativebc.ca/massive-ndp-land-transfers-could-total-up-to-24-million-acres/
- **Provincial government's own public position** (per Epoch Times
  reporting, Aug 11–12, 2026): the province stated "public access and
  recreational opportunities for the provincial parks would still be
  safeguarded under the terms of the treaties." Source:
  https://www.theepochtimes.com/world/newly-revealed-bc-ndp-first-nations-land-transfers-amount-to-nearly-135000-hectares-6073480
- **Earlier, contemporaneous CBC reporting (2023)** on the same Sooke
  Mountain/Discovery Island parks question indicated they were expected to
  "remain open to the public," managed by First Nations post-transfer
  (could not be independently re-verified in this pass — CBC's own page
  returned an access error on fetch; flagged for follow-up verification
  before citing as settled fact). Search-result summary only:
  https://www.cbc.ca/lite/story/1.6732593 — **treat as unverified pending
  direct confirmation.**

**For the report:** this is squarely a "contested claim, not yet resolved
by an actual completed treaty" situation — exactly the kind of thing the
"already-effective" precedent in sections 1–4 above is meant to inform,
without itself being decisive. The honest framing is that BC's completed
treaties show public-access guarantees are the norm in treaty text, which
argues against the Conservative claim being automatically true for
Te'mexw — but it is also true (per Tla'amin/Ahgykson and the Nisga'a road
clause) that specific parcels can and do get carved out with reduced
access, so "under no legal obligation" is not a fact that can be
dismissed out of hand either without seeing the actual (not yet public,
per the KB doc) Te'mexw agreement text.
