# Sharon Polsky, MAPP -- Privacy Enforcement & Bill C-27 Brief

## Who Is She

**Sharon Polsky** is President of the **Privacy and Access Council of Canada (PACC)** -- Canada's national certifying body for access and privacy professionals. Founded 2002, independent, non-partisan, not funded by government or industry.

- **30+ years** advising corporations, governments, Parliamentary and Senate committees
- **MAPP** = Member of the Association of Privacy Professionals (PACC's professional designation)
- Privacy by Design Ambassador (appointed 2011 by Ann Cavoukian)
- Vice-Chair, CIO Strategy Council Technical Committees for Privacy & Access Control Standards
- Contributing writer, Centre for International Governance Innovation (CIGI)
- Former adjunct instructor, University of Calgary and SAIT Polytechnic (15 years)
- Twitter/X: [@PolskySays](https://twitter.com/PolskySays)

## What She Has Argued -- The Core Thesis

### 1. Bill C-27 Was Fundamentally Broken

Polsky's position was unambiguous: **do not enact Bill C-27**.

> "Bill C-27 is bad law because it would undermine everyone's privacy, including children's."

Her specific criticisms:

- **Wrong framing:** Defines people as "consumers" not rights-holders; prioritizes "data sharing to foster commerce" over rights
- **De-identification is fiction:** Larger AI datasets make re-identification more likely every day; pharma using your DNA without consent is "fine" if de-identified
- **No government AI transparency:** Only regulates private sector, not government use of AI
- **No executive liability:** Only organizations can be fined, not executives personally
- **Consent model unchanged:** Still allows "vague language and generalities" in privacy policies
- **No right to be forgotten:** Can't control what data is shared or with whom
- **Won't stop data brokers:** $300B+ global industry untouched
- **Creates harmful bureaucracy:** The Tribunal (see below)

### 2. The Tribunal Model Is the Problem

This is the exact issue from the tweet context. Here's how it works and why Polsky opposes it:

**What Bill C-27 proposed (Part 2 -- Personal Information and Data Protection Tribunal Act):**

```
Breach occurs
    --> OPC investigates, makes finding
        --> Organization can appeal to NEW Tribunal
            --> Tribunal reviews, makes decision
                --> Only AFTER Tribunal exhausts process...
                    --> Individual can finally pursue private right of action
```

**What a former Privacy Commissioner said:** This process would **extend enforcement by 7-10 YEARS** before an individual could seek recourse.

**Polsky's position:** The Tribunal is "novel among data protection authorities" worldwide. No other country has one. It adds a bureaucratic layer that **benefits offending organizations** by delaying consequences.

**What she wants instead:**

```
Breach occurs
    --> OPC investigates with ORDER-MAKING POWER
        --> OPC can impose penalties directly (like GDPR model)
            --> Organization appeals to Federal Court (existing system)
                --> Individual has IMMEDIATE private right of action
```

### 3. The OPC Enforcement Gap (Tweet Context)

This directly connects to the tweet about Loblaw, Telus Digital, CIRO, and PowerSchool:

**Current reality (PIPEDA):**
- OPC can investigate and make recommendations
- OPC **cannot fine anyone** or issue binding orders
- OPC can write a letter asking organizations to "do better"
- Organizations face **zero financial penalty** for non-compliance
- Companies treat any fines (when they exist) as "lunch money" -- tax-deductible line items

**Bill C-27 would have:**
- Introduced fines up to **3% of global revenue** (or $10M, whichever greater) for CPPA violations
- Up to **5% of global revenue** (or $25M) for the most serious offences
- Given OPC order-making power -- **subject to review by the Tribunal**
- Created a private right of action -- **only after Tribunal process exhausted**

**Bill C-27 died January 2025. No replacement tabled.**

### 4. Her Specific Recommendations to Parliament

**On enforcement:**
- Grant OPC **direct enforcement power** across both public AND private sectors
- Allow **personal fines for executives**, not just organizations
- Create **immediate private right of action** without Tribunal delay
- Model enforcement on GDPR / Sarbanes-Oxley style accountability

**On consent:**
- Reverse the onus: corporations must prove compliance, not individuals
- Create a **public compliance index** where organizations demonstrate adherence
- Require organizations' privacy officers to hold recognized PACC certification

**On technology:**
- Mandate **pre-market regulatory oversight** for privacy-invasive technologies
- Require **Privacy Impact Assessments** before deploying surveillance tools
- Make spyware unlawful except in specific exceptional circumstances
- Enshrine privacy as a **fundamental human right** in operative law (not just preambles)

**On children:**
- Bill C-27 doesn't define "sensitive information" yet claims children's data is sensitive
- Companies should not be able to commercialize children's biometric or location data
- Need specific, enumerated protections -- don't leave it to regulators to define later

## How These Models Compare

| Feature | Current (PIPEDA) | Bill C-27 (died) | Polsky's Model | GDPR (EU) |
|---------|-----------------|-------------------|----------------|-----------|
| **Fines** | $0 | Up to 5% global rev | Up to 5%+ global rev | Up to 4% global rev |
| **OPC orders** | No | Yes, but appealable to Tribunal | Yes, appealable to Federal Court | Yes (DPAs have direct power) |
| **Private right of action** | No | Yes, after Tribunal exhausts | Yes, immediate | Yes |
| **Executive liability** | No | No | Yes | Yes (in practice) |
| **Consent model** | Opt-out | Plain language (same onus) | Reverse onus on corporations | Explicit, informed, revocable |
| **Enforcement timeline** | Never (recommendations only) | 7-10 years (Tribunal path) | Months (direct OPC orders) | Months (direct DPA orders) |
| **Privacy as right** | Preamble only | Added to purpose clause | Operative fundamental right | Charter of Fundamental Rights |
