Foreign Direct Investment Inflows to Canada

Source: Statistics Canada, Table 36-10-0026-01 — Balance of International Payments, FDI Flows by NAICS Industry · 2007–2025

Canada recorded $93.6 billion in foreign investment inflows in 2025 — the highest total since 2007. That headline is accurate. What it omits is the sector breakdown: 63% of those inflows went into extraction, financial structuring, or foreign acquisitions of existing Canadian businesses. Only 37% reached manufacturing and trade infrastructure — the sectors that create new jobs and industrial capacity. This pattern is not new: extractive capital has exceeded productive capital in 14 of the 19 years since 2007. The charts and findings below examine what the composition shows and what policy mechanisms exist to change it.

How to read the KISS classification

Not all foreign investment is equal. Some capital builds lasting Canadian productive capacity — it creates jobs, transfers technology, and expands industrial output. Other capital extracts value, gains control of strategic resources, or represents financial structuring that flows through Canada without leaving roots behind. The KISS split groups all six StatCan NAICS sectors into two buckets to make this distinction visible.

Green — Productive
Manufacturing — builds factories, industrial capacity, and skilled employment in Canada
Trade & Transportation — logistics, wholesale, and distribution infrastructure
Red — Extractive / Control Risk
Energy & Mining — foreign ownership of Canadian oil, gas, and mineral resources; value typically repatriated abroad
Finance & Insurance — complex financial flows that may represent tax structuring or short-term capital positioning
Management of Companies — foreign holding company acquisitions; control of existing Canadian businesses transferred abroad with no new capacity created
Other Industries — residual category including real estate and professional services

Findings

01

The Record Total Is Real — The Composition Isn't What It Looks Like

The $93.6B 2025 total is the highest since 2007, but the productive share was just $34.8B (37%). The other $58.8B went into extraction, financial structuring, and foreign acquisitions of existing Canadian businesses.

02

2024's Manufacturing Record Was Tariff Arbitrage, Not a Structural Shift

Manufacturing spiked to $43.1B in 2024 — nearly triple any prior year — as companies raced to establish Canadian capacity ahead of US tariffs. In 2025 it collapsed to $11.2B. The underlying composition did not change.

03

Management of Companies Consistently Transfers Canadian Business Ownership Abroad

This category records foreign holding companies acquiring existing Canadian businesses — no new jobs, no new factories. It has run at $14–15B in peak years (2014, 2015, 2022, 2025) with no government disclosure.

04

Extractive Capital Exceeding Productive Is the Baseline, Not an Exception

Red exceeded green in 14 of 19 years. The five exceptions all involve a one-off spike (tariff arbitrage) or crash (COVID, energy divestment) — not a structural shift in Canada's FDI composition.

Policy Recommendations
Six mechanisms to improve FDI composition and transparency
Near-term regulatory action
⚖️

Lower the Investment Canada Act review threshold for company acquisitions

The net-benefit review threshold is $1.075B — the vast majority of Management of Companies acquisitions pass unscrutinized. A mandatory public-interest test for acquisitions above $100M would bring ISED review to the $14–15B/yr category. Precedent: Australia's FIRB expanded mandatory review to all sensitive-sector acquisitions in 2021.
Feasibility — Medium
Requires ICA amendment; achievable in one session.
Impact — Direct
Applies public-interest test to the category that currently receives none.
⛏️

Condition resource FDI approvals on domestic value-add requirements

Energy & Mining FDI transfers ownership of Canadian resources; profits repatriate abroad. ICA s.23.1 already authorizes binding approval conditions — NRCan should enforce minimum processing ratios and reinvestment thresholds. Norway captured resource revenue at the national level instead of repatriating to foreign shareholders.
Feasibility — Medium
Authority exists; requires sustained monitoring capacity.
Impact — Systemic
Shifts resource monetization toward value-added processing.
🏭

Deploy retention instruments for 2024 manufacturing capacity

The 2024 manufacturing surge was tariff arbitrage — plants are mobile and will redomicile when the window closes. Budget 2026 should deploy multi-year SIF retention instruments before facilities leave. The US CHIPS Act committed US$52B on the same logic.
Feasibility — High
SIF mechanism exists and has been used for retention cases.
Impact — Critical
2024-vintage facilities at material risk of redomiciling in 2–3 years.
Systemic transparency & accountability
📊

Require productive-share disclosure in every FDI communication

Every Budget and Fall Economic Statement announces total FDI as a win — none disclose the sector split. Parliament should direct the Minister of Finance to publish the annual green/red breakdown alongside every FDI headline. UK ONS and EU Eurostat both publish this as standard practice.
Feasibility — High
Data already compiled; this is a disclosure decision only.
Impact — Preventive
Closes the gap that allows selective headline reporting.
🏛️

Establish a Parliamentary FDI composition KPI with annual review

The productive share has averaged 39% since 2007 with no upward trend and no accountability mechanism. A Finance Committee motion should establish an annual FDI Composition Report — modelled on the Fiscal Monitor — with a target productive share and annual ministerial appearance. Australia's FIRB publishes exactly this report.
Feasibility — High
Committee motion; no legislation required.
Impact — Systemic
Creates accountability infrastructure that has never existed.
🤝

Incorporate productive-capacity benchmarks in future FIPAs

Canada's FIPAs protect investor rights but set no contribution requirements. Future agreements should include binding commitments: employment targets, R&D ratios, or technology transfer obligations as conditions of investor protection. Bilateral investment treaties in Southeast Asia provide the structural model.
Feasibility — Low
Long-horizon; existing FIPAs are slow to renegotiate.
Impact — Systemic
Changes foundational terms of investment attraction over 10–20 years.
