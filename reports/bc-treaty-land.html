The report

An objective, source-based data investigation · August 2026

The Land Ledger

A social media post went viral claiming British Columbia had committed 334,000 acres of Crown land to First Nations — including Hatley Castle — with limited or no public access, and no public say. We went and read the actual documents: five years of Public Accounts, the treaty maps, the fact sheets, the treaty text itself. The core number checks out. Almost everything built around it — the trend, the Victoria parcels, the access claim — needs a footnote the original post didn't include.

Before you read

How this investigation is built, and what it does not claim

Treaty land is one of the most consequential and least-understood parts of BC public policy, and a subject that invites strong feelings on every side. The standards we hold ourselves to:

  • Primary documents only for every headline figure — five years of BC Public Accounts PDFs, BC Treaty Commission Final Agreement fact sheets, and official treaty text, extracted directly rather than taken from screenshots or news summaries.
  • We correct the viral claim's numbers where they're wrong, and say so plainly where they're right. The 135,425 ha figure and the 47%-of-Metro-Vancouver comparison both check out. The "rising" framing and the Hatley Castle attribution don't.
  • We keep document scopes distinct. Provincial Crown land, federal Crown land, existing reserve land, and private land are four different legal categories. Conflating them is where most of the confusion in this story originated.
  • We do not adopt policy opinions from any side. This report describes what land is committed, to whom, under what process, and what happens to public access — not whether the treaty process itself should exist or be reformed.
  • We report live, unresolved disputes as unresolved — including an active First Nation-vs-First Nation legal claim over one of the most visible parcels in this story.

Chapter 1 · The numbers

135,425 hectares — verified, but not the whole story

Key correction

−2.3% The province's committed-treaty-land total has fallen, not risen, for two straight fiscal years — from a 2023/24 peak of 138,591 ha to 135,425 ha today.

The most-cited number from the viral post is accurate. The "committed and growing" framing built around it isn't.

Every year, BC's Public Accounts disclose a running list — Note 28 — of provincial Crown land committed under active treaty negotiations. As of 2025/26, that's 135,425 hectares across 20 First Nations and treaty tables, verified word-for-word against the official government PDF. What the viral framing leaves out: the number peaked in 2023/24 and has been falling since, and it's dwarfed by the 232,746 hectares already transferred, land in hand, under BC's four completed treaties — about 1.7× more than what's still pending.

Note 28 total committed hectares by fiscal year (BC Public Accounts, 2021/22–2025/26).

The capital transfer money that usually comes with a treaty is disclosed as, "in most cases," a federal cost — not a provincial one. The land is BC's to commit; the cheque is mostly Ottawa's.

Chapter 2 · Victoria / Te’mexw case study

Victoria's fine print: what's actually new, and what Songhees already holds

Key correction

$0 Hatley Castle is federal land (Department of National Defence) — it is not counted in the 135,425 ha provincial figure at all.

The viral post's map is real and traced to its source — a Ministry of Indigenous Relations poster, June 2026. But it conflates two different documents with two different legal scopes.

The Te’mexw table — five Nations (Malahat, Scia’new, Snaw-naw-as, Songhees, T’Sou-ke) — accounts for 1,916.7 hectares of the province-wide total, spread across their entire South Island territory, not concentrated in Victoria. Of what's actually shown in the viral Victoria map, only a handful of small parcels — a parking lot beside the Legislature, a waterfront dock, a strip of Discovery Island park — are genuine new provincial commitments. Hatley Park (federal land, ~229 ha) and Songhees' existing reserve holdings are something else entirely — and Hatley Park's proposed transfer is now contested by a separate legal claim from Esquimalt Nation, a distinct First Nation, filed March 27, 2026 and still unresolved.

Esquimalt Nation and Songhees Nation are both successor communities of the Lekwungen people, split into separate Indian Act bands in the late 1800s. Their dispute over Hatley Park is First Nation vs. First Nation, not government vs. First Nation.

Chapter 3 · Public access

After the ink dries: what happens to public access

Key correction

2 of 4 BC's four already-effective modern treaties contain a documented, named example of public access being narrowed — but all four also carry explicit access-preserving guarantees.

The "no public access" claim is real precedent, not invention — but it's not the norm either. Both things are true at once.

Treaty settlement land becomes ordinary fee-simple private property — the same legal category as any house or farm in BC — and every one of the four completed treaties (Nisga’a, Tsawwassen, Maa-nulth, Tla’amin) writes in explicit public-access guarantees beyond that default. (Yale's Final Agreement was ratified but BC's own Public Accounts say no effective date has been agreed — see Chapter 1 — so it's excluded from this already-effective set.) But real, disclosed exceptions exist too: Tla’amin's treaty designated all of Ahgykson (Harwood Island) as private land with restricted access, on the treaty's own effective date. Nisga’a's treaty lets the Nation close certain non-village roads. Both are negotiated, disclosed terms — not surprises buried in fine print.

BC's existing reserve land (~473,485 ha) is already about 3.5× larger than the pending 135,425 ha commitment. Once transferred, treaty settlement land leaves the reserve system entirely and becomes private land.
