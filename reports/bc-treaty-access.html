After the Ink Dries

Source: Nisga'a, Tsawwassen, Maa-nulth & Tla'amin Final Agreements (primary treaty text) · BC Treaty Commission · BC Public Accounts 2025/26, Note 28

The tweet that started this story claimed some treaty lands "could also have limited or NO public access" — without a source. This chapter checks that claim the only way it can honestly be checked: against BC's four modern treaties that are actually in effect (Nisga'a, Tsawwassen, Maa-nulth, Tla'amin) — not against speculation about deals still being negotiated, and not Yale, whose Final Agreement was ratified but which Note 28 itself says has no agreed effective date (see Chapter 1). The finding is genuinely mixed. Across all four, settlement land converts to ordinary fee-simple private land — the same legal category as any house or farm in BC — and every one of the four treaties contains explicit clauses that preserve public access beyond what an ordinary landowner would owe anyone. But real, named, treaty-text-documented cases of access being narrowed also exist: the Nisga'a Nation can close certain roads to the public, and Tla'amin designated an entire island as private land with restricted access, on the treaty's own effective date. This is a negotiated, disclosed balance — not a blank check for closure, and not a guarantee of blanket openness either.

What a treaty settlement actually converts land into

None of the four treaties below create a special new category of land exempt from BC's ordinary property system. Settlement land is converted to fee simple ownership — the Nisga'a Final Agreement's own language calls it "the largest estate known in law" — registered in the provincial Land Title Office exactly like any privately owned house, farm, or forestry lot in BC. It sits alongside the Nation's own law-making authority over that land (zoning, planning, development), but the underlying legal category is not novel and is not reserve land.

It is also not an "extinguishment" of Aboriginal rights, in the way 19th- and early-20th-century historic treaties worked. The BC Treaty Commission's own FAQ is explicit: "extinguishment and surrender of rights, in form or result, do not have any place in modern-day Crown–Indigenous relations." Modern treaties use a modification model instead — the Nisga'a treaty states that Nisga'a Aboriginal rights, including title, "are modified, and continue as modified, as set out in that Agreement." The exchange is defined, certain rights across a settlement area, in place of standing uncertainty about how Aboriginal title applies across a much larger traditional territory.

Checking the claim, entity by entity

BC Treaty Commission Sets the process
The independent body overseeing the 6-stage negotiation process every one of BC's treaty tables sits within, from Statement of Intent through Implementation. The legal default it produces — fee simple, registered like any private parcel — is the same for every completed treaty, not something negotiated differently case by case.

Nisga'a Lisims Government Real, named restriction
Under its own treaty (effective May 11, 2000), the Nisga'a Nation may close to the public any Nisga'a road that is not within Nisga'a Village Lands (Chapter 7, para 60(1)) — a real, in-force clause, and the clearest documented precedent for the tweet's claim. It sits alongside a separate guarantee (Chapter 6) that Nisga'a Public Lands stay open to reasonable public recreational access. This is the Nation exercising an authority it negotiated for itself, not something imposed on it from outside.

Tla'amin Nation Real, named restriction
Under its own treaty (effective April 5, 2016), the entirety of Ahgykson (Harwood Island) was designated Tla'amin Private Land with restricted public access — on the treaty's own effective date. That designation is disclosed directly in the treaty text itself, not a surprise sprung on anyone afterward. Tla'amin also guarantees public recreational access elsewhere on Tla'amin Public Lands and on the stretch of the Sunshine Coast Trail that crosses its land.

Maa-nulth First Nations Clearest counter-example
The clearest documented case of access simply matching ordinary private-land rules: the Final Agreement states each Maa-nulth Nation has "the same rights and obligations in respect of public access... as other owners of estates in fee simple." As part of the same treaty, Thunderbird's Nest (T'iitsk'in Paawats, ~2,185 ha) was added to protected status — pulled out of the working forest land base — an access and conservation gain, not a loss.

The live, unresolved Te'mexw dispute Unverified — context only
Te'mexw is not yet an effective treaty (Stage 5 of 6), so it sits outside the "already-effective" precedent above — but it's the live dispute that the original viral post's map was actually about. On Aug 11, 2026, the BC Conservative Party publicly claimed the Te'mexw Treaty Association would be "under no legal obligation to continue to allow public access to their newly acquired parks." The province's own public position, per Epoch Times reporting the same news cycle (Aug 11–12, 2026), was that access "would still be safeguarded under the terms of the treaties." Neither claim is independently verified here — the actual Te'mexw treaty text is not yet public. Presented as two competing claims, not as settled fact either way.

"The Te'mexw Treaty Association is under no legal obligation to continue to allow public access to their newly acquired parks." — BC Conservative Party statement, Aug 11, 2026
"Public access and recreational opportunities for the provincial parks would still be safeguarded under the terms of the treaties." — BC provincial government, per The Epoch Times, Aug 11–12, 2026

Separate context — do not conflate: Cowichan Tribes v. Canada (Attorney General), 2025 BCSC 1490 (decided Aug 7, 2025) found that Aboriginal title can persist over land already held in fee simple, in a Richmond, BC case. That case involves land that was never part of any modern BC treaty — the Cowichan have never signed one. It's a different, unrelated legal question (title uncertainty on non-treaty land), not a precedent about what happens to treaty settlement land. If anything, it illustrates that treaty settlement — which converts land to fee simple with the Nation's own agreement, in exchange for defined certainty — is one of the few mechanisms that forecloses this kind of title uncertainty, rather than creating it.
