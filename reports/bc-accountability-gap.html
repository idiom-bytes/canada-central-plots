The Provincial and Federal Wedge Dividing BC's Sovereignty

Federal Indigenous spending, First Nations financial transparency and BC land agreements, laid side by side. Here is what the records show, and what they don't.

By Canada Central · · Financial-filing data recomputed from federal records as of · Data, method and sources

British Columbians are voting while three things are happening at once, and almost none of them is on a campaign poster:

  1. 1 · SpendingOttawa's yearly spending on Indigenous programs is several times what it was a decade ago.
  2. 2 · TransparencyA federal law that requires every First Nation council to publish its audited finances within 120 days of year-end has been on the books since 2013, but the federal government stopped enforcing it in December 2015.
  3. 3 · LandThe BC government has committed Crown land to First Nations through treaties and other agreements, some of which became public only through news reports and freedom-of-information requests.

This report sets those facts side by side. It recomputes the financial-transparency numbers from the federal government's own portal rather than relying on screenshots, separates what is documented from what is merely claimed, and includes each government's own explanation. It takes no position on whether treaties, land return or reconciliation are right. The question it asks is narrower: can the public see what is being done in its name?

1 · The ruleA rule that stopped being enforced

The First Nations Financial Transparency Act (FNFTA) was passed in March 2013. It requires each First Nation it covers to publish audited financial statements and a schedule of what its chief and councillors are paid, within 120 days of the fiscal year-end (March 31), and to keep them online for ten years. The point, in the law's own terms, is that members can see how money is spent. Ottawa also posts the documents it receives on a public portal.

Ottawa could once withhold funding or go to court if a nation did not publish. In December 2015, Indigenous Affairs Minister Carolyn Bennett directed the department to end all discretionary compliance measures, restore about $12 million withheld from some 43 communities, and suspend court actions. The Assembly of First Nations welcomed the move; critics said it gutted the law. The Act remains in force. It is simply not enforced.

Because Ottawa publishes the filing date for every document, compliance can be measured directly. Counting a nation as on time only if both required documents arrived within 120 days of year-end, the picture is stark.

The widely shared Canada Spends chart, which counted about 40% of First Nations as having filed their latest statements, was accurate for the moment it was drawn in early 2026. Late filings keep arriving, and as of about % have filed both documents for 2024-25. The deeper finding is that only of did so on time. The recompute matches Canada Spends' on-time counts almost exactly for 2013-14 to 2018-19, which gives some confidence in the method.

2 · British ColumbiaLate, not absent: where BC stands

BC has more First Nations than any other province: are listed on the federal portal, of which appear to be covered by the Act. (Twelve treaty or self-governing nations, including the four Nisga'a villages, Tsawwassen and the five Maa-nulth nations, have never filed because they appear to fall outside it.)

BC's nations file more often than the national average: % have filed both documents for 2024-25, versus % nationally. But on-time filing is just as rare, at % ( nations). Among BC nations that filed both documents for 2024-25, the median was about days past the deadline.

A June 2026 Western Standard story added a related finding. An access-to-information request asked Crown-Indigenous Relations for a summary of the latest documented contact with each BC First Nation. The department replied that it held "none responsive" records. The headline read that Ottawa has "no record of contact" with BC's First Nations.

That overstates it. Access-to-information law covers records that exist; a department is not required to build a spreadsheet. What the reply does show is that no consolidated contact log exists. The same article's count (69 BC nations had filed their latest statements as of June) has already risen. It is also worth noting that the financial filings on the federal portal are themselves a documented form of contact, and the department has published them for over a decade.

“A $12 billion department built to manage ‘nation-to-nation relationships’ says it has no records of actually engaging with First Nations in British Columbia.”

3 · The moneyWhat Ottawa spends

The scale depends on the definition, and the two best-known measures differ by about $23 billion. Canada Spends adds up the budgets of Indigenous Services Canada and Crown-Indigenous Relations: from $10.3 billion in 2014 to $63.9 billion in 2024, a cumulative $243.8 billion over the decade. That series includes one-time settlement accounting, for instance a roughly $23 billion First Nations child-welfare settlement.

The Fraser Institute's 2026 study, by Tom Flanagan and Nathaniel Li, excludes settlements but counts Indigenous programs in every department. It finds spending rising from $10.3 billion to $40.8 billion (inflation-adjusted, 2014-15 to 2024-25), seven times faster than federal spending overall. For BC, they estimate about $6.3 billion in 2024-25, or $20,306 per Indigenous person, by apportioning national spending by population.

One viral version of this story says $243.8 billion "went to First Nations groups." That is not what the number measures. It is departmental spending on health, housing, water, education, child welfare, land claims and settlement liabilities for or on behalf of Indigenous people, not a total of cheques to band councils.

A second shared graphic ranks per-person federal spending, with "Indigenous priorities" at $1,251, above defence ($1,010), employment insurance ($678) and public safety ($663). Its ranking is real, but the screenshot has no title, year or stated denominator, and its 5.8% share does not match Canada Spends' own 12.3% for the two departments. 4 · The outcomesWhat happened to First Nations health

It is natural to ask what that spending has bought. The best official evidence for BC comes from the First Nations Health Authority and the Provincial Health Officer, who track 22 wellness indicators through 2030. Their latest report, published in August 2024, covers data to 2021.

On the headline measure, life expectancy at birth for Status First Nations people in BC was years in 2013, in 2019 and in 2021. For other BC residents it stayed near 83 throughout, so the gap grew from to years. All-cause mortality rose from 117.3 per 10,000 in 2017 to 156.0 in 2021.

The timing matters for how the finding is read. About two and a half years of the fall (2013 to 2019) came before the pandemic. But most of it came in 2020 and 2021, and the health authorities attribute that mainly to the toxic drug crisis and COVID-19. In 2023, First Nations people died of unregulated drugs at more than six times the rate of other British Columbians, and accounted for 17.8% of BC's toxic-drug deaths while being under 4% of the population. Other BC residents also lost about a year of life expectancy between 2019 and 2021.

The picture is not uniformly negative. Infant mortality, high-school completion and youth suicide all moved in the right direction, while diabetes, birth weight and mortality worsened.

    5 · The landThe land BC has committed

    BC's audited public accounts disclose how much provincial Crown land it has committed to transfer under treaties: hectares in 2025/26, about % of Metro Vancouver's area. The figure is down from its 2023/24 peak, and it is separate from the roughly hectares already transferred under BC's four completed treaties.

    Treaties are public, voted on by the nations and legislated. The more contested category is everything outside them. A confidential briefing dated September 10, 2026, obtained by Northern Beat (Rob Shaw), says that "presently, there are approximately 400 parcels committed for transfer through Treaty and reconciliation agreements." It does not list the parcels or their total size. It also describes a plan to amend the Forest Act in spring 2027 so that forest companies are paid less, or nothing, when land is removed from their tenures for First Nations purposes.

    The BC NDP says the document is a "set of draft ideas" from the ministry, and that some of the 400 parcels are already public through agreements such as the Lake Babine and K'omoks incremental treaties and the 2023 Cheslatta land transfer. The briefing's slide deck, however, says cabinet approved a request for decision in July. According to Northern Beat, voters have not been told of it during the campaign.

    Three agreements show what "reconciliation agreement" can mean in practice.

    “Making agreements with First Nations is how the government resolves outstanding land claims and brings certainty to the land base. This has been the approach for decades and is critical to unlocking the economic potential of the province.”

    — BC NDP statement to Northern Beat

    Both sides of this argument have a point. Settling claims by agreement is the route courts and governments have urged for decades, and it can bring certainty and investment. The criticism is about process: the Tahltan agreement, covering about 11% of the province, was revealed through an FOI request, while the minister told the Legislature that anyone could find press releases on it by using Google. The terms, the federal role, and whether it needs legislative approval remain undisclosed.

    6 · The lawAboriginal title and private property

    A widely shared post by lawyer Keith Wilson states that 95% of BC is under an Aboriginal title claim, that the Cowichan principles mean title "trumps" the fee simple held by landowners, and that residents "may soon no longer own their homes and businesses." Each part needs separating.

    The 95% figure could not be traced to any BC government, treaty commission or court source; it appears to originate in the post itself. Territories asserted by different nations overlap heavily and mostly have not been tested, so how much of BC is "claimed" depends on whose map is used. A popular language-and-territory map of BC's First Nations is sometimes cited for this; it shows cultural areas, not title claims, and cannot support such a calculation.

    The Cowichan ruling (Justice Barbara Young, BC Supreme Court, August 7 2025) found Aboriginal title over a portion of a roughly 1,846-acre claim area of riverfront land in Richmond. The judgment shows the title boundary on a map; secondary reports put it at about . The court found that Crown grants of fee simple on that land unjustifiably infringe Aboriginal title, but it declared only specific Canada- and Richmond-owned lots invalid, and suspended that declaration for 18 months. It did not void private owners' titles. The plaintiffs did not seek that, and the private owners are not parties to the case. BC and Canada are instead ordered to negotiate in good faith. On how the two coexist, the judge wrote that title and private fee simple “will exist in the same land at the same time until future litigation or negotiation further modifies them” (para. 2208).

    Where that leaves homeowners

      The honest summary is uncertainty, not loss. No private home or business has been ruled void, every party is appealing, and BC's own position is that a private property right sits above Aboriginal title. Commentators note that BC's stated position now differs from the one it took at trial, and how title and private ownership coexist will not be settled until the appeals are decided. The unsigned Tahltan agreement and the 2025 Haida title agreement, which recognized title over an entire archipelago including private property, show governments choosing negotiated recognition without waiting for courts to test claims.

      7 · The fundersWho funds the agenda

      A recurring claim is that outside money shapes which projects get built in BC and how First Nations are represented. What is documented is narrower than the claim, but real. Over two decades, US charitable foundations have funded First Nation-linked conservation and advocacy on the BC coast, including the Great Bear Rainforest agreements and the creation of the Coastal First Nations alliance, according to Northern Beat's reporting on US tax filings gathered by researcher Vivian Krause.

      The documented funders are the Rockefeller Brothers Fund, the Gordon and Betty Moore Foundation, the Packard and Hewlett foundations, the Nature Conservancy, Tides Canada (now MakeWay) and, in recent years, MacKenzie Scott. No source reviewed for this report documents funding to BC First Nations from George Soros or Open Society Foundations, and the article most often cited for this story never mentions him.

      What the filings and reporting document

        "First Nations in name only"?

        • Coastal First Nations is an alliance of roughly 8–10 of about 70 coastal communities, created in 2004 with US foundation money. It is not itself an Indian Act band or a treaty government.
        • Band councils, treaty nations and self-governing nations are separate legal entities with their own members, elections and, in most cases, FNFTA duties.
        • Foreign philanthropic funding is lawful and publicly reported in the funders' tax filings. Whether it should influence land-use negotiations is a political question; the funding itself is not evidence of wrongdoing by any nation.

        9 · ConclusionWhat this adds up to

        What the record establishes

        1. The financial-transparency rule is law, but is not enforced: of First Nations filed on time in 2024-25, and BC's record is similar ( of ). Most nations do eventually file.
        2. Federal Indigenous spending rose sharply by any measure, from about $10 billion to between $41 and $64 billion a year, but no public data ties that spending to outcomes nation by nation.
        3. BC First Nations life expectancy fell by about 8.5 years from its peak. Most of the fall came in 2020–21 and is attributed to COVID-19 and the toxic drug crisis, and some indicators improved. The data ends in 2021.
        4. BC has committed 135,425 hectares under treaties and roughly 400 parcels overall. The parcel list and total size have not been published, and the key document was obtained by a news outlet, not released by the government.

        What it does not establish

        • That any individual First Nation has misused funds. Late or missing filings can reflect small size, limited capacity or posting delays.
        • That spending levels or the transparency lapse caused the health decline.
        • That private homeowners are losing title, that 95% of BC is claimed, or that any specific foreign donor controls an outcome.

        What would close the gap

        • Decide on the Act. Enforce the transparency law or amend it openly. An unenforced law helps neither members nor taxpayers.
        • Publish the land list. Release the locations and total area behind the roughly 400 parcels, and disclose agreements before elections, not after.
        • Keep a contact and filing record. A department that spends about $12 billion a year should be able to produce a list of its most recent interaction with each nation.
        • Report spending by purpose. Show settlements separately from services, and per nation where privacy allows.
        • Publish the next health update on time and link outcomes to specific programs, so that the debate can move from totals to results.

        Whichever way voters lean on reconciliation, the same test applies: members of First Nations, taxpayers and neighbours all benefit from being able to see where money and land are going. Right now, much of that can only be seen by those who dig.
