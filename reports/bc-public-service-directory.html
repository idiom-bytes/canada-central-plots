The Last Census

Source: BC Government Directory, last public snapshot April–July 2026 · Public access restricted 2026-08-18

For years, anyone could search dir.gov.bc.ca to find who worked where inside British Columbia's government — every ministry, branch, and job title, in one public directory published in some form since at least 1930, online since 2007. On August 18, 2026, without warning, the province restricted public access — two days ahead of its own announced August 20 cutoff — citing cybersecurity threats and employee privacy. No aggregate replacement went up in its place: no public table of headcount by ministry, no published workforce breakdown. This report uses a preserved snapshot of the directory, captured via the Wayback Machine before the takedown and stripped of every individually-identifying field (name, phone, email, street address), to answer a question the government no longer publishes an easy answer to: who actually works in B.C.'s public service, and what do they do? It also checks a natural follow-up: is B.C. actually unusual here, or does every province now do this?

Who is responsible?

Ministry of Citizens' Services Owns dir.gov.bc.ca
This ministry operates the directory platform and made the call to restrict public access, disabling it on August 18, 2026 without advance warning. A ministry spokesperson said the move followed "proactive advice from law enforcement partners, including the RCMP," and that "cyber threat actors" had been taking advantage of the directory's open access. That's a real, legitimate operational concern — a bulk-downloadable roster of 22,000+ names, direct phone numbers, and office locations is a genuine phishing and social-engineering risk. But the same decision removed the only public way to answer basic accountability questions — how big is a given ministry, how is staff distributed across functions — without replacing it with any aggregate, non-identifying alternative.

BC Public Service Agency Workforce steward
The Agency is the province's official steward of public-service workforce data and already publishes some aggregate statistics (e.g. annual headcount totals) through its own channels. It has not published a ministry-by-ministry or function-by-function breakdown comparable to what the directory made freely searchable.

The licensing question
The original directory extract was published under the Open Government Licence – British Columbia, which explicitly excludes "Personal Information" from its grant but does not exclude business contact information (name, title, work phone, work email) under FOIPPA's standard legal definition. So the underlying data was very likely within the licence's scope even before this report's de-identification step. The province's cybersecurity/privacy rationale for the takedown is a distinct, legitimate operational-risk argument — not a claim that the data was mishandled or improperly licensed.

How this report handles the data

Every field that could identify a specific person — first/last name, direct phone and fax numbers, email address, street address, and postal code — was permanently removed before this analysis. What remains is job title, ministry, branch, office city/province, and a synthetic record ID. See the By Job Function view below for one honest limitation of that trade-off: with names and other identifiers gone, categorizing 22,000+ free-text job titles into functions relies on keyword matching against the title alone, and roughly 30% don't cleanly match a specific category (labeled "Other / Unclassified" rather than forced into one). Office city is also missing for about a third of individual records in the source directory itself — not a result of de-identification.

This is a single snapshot (captured 2026-04-09, with a small number of records refreshed through 2026-07-14), not a time series. There is no earlier public export to compare it against, and with the live directory now down, there is currently no way to produce a fresher one.

Is B.C. actually alone in this? Not literally — see the Provincial Comparison view below. Two jurisdictions (Manitoba, Northwest Territories) don't appear to have ever built a public directory, and Nunavut restricts its directory to signed-in staff. What makes B.C. distinct is that it's the only jurisdiction found where an existing, decades-old public directory was deliberately taken offline in 2026 — and provincial media directly named Ontario, Saskatchewan, and New Brunswick as far more open by comparison.
