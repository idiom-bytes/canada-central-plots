The investigation

A three-chapter investigation · Updated August 2026

Canada Is Letting Itself Burn

Wildfire is treated as a natural disaster Canada responds to, not a national number Canada tracks. This investigation follows that gap through three layers: what one province's own records show about how fires get recorded, what the rest of the country's records show once you look past British Columbia, and what all of it adds up to in carbon — a quantity nobody puts on Canada's climate ledger at all. Each chapter is a full, sourced dashboard; this page is the map between them.

Before you read

How this investigation is built

Every figure below comes from a primary dataset — BC Wildfire Service's live incident records, the Canadian National Fire Database (Canadian Forest Service / NRCan), and Environment and Climate Change Canada's National Inventory Report — fetched directly by a pipeline script, not typed in by hand. Where no official multi-year dataset exists (wildfire-specific CO2 estimates, avoided-emissions modeling), that gap is disclosed explicitly rather than papered over, and any scenario math is shown in full, labeled as an estimate, not a government figure.

  • This is not an arson story. A 2023 PolitiFact fact-check rated a viral "Canada wildfire arson epidemic" claim False, and this investigation's own numbers agree: humans start the majority of fires by count, but only around 6% of the area that actually burns. The story here is what doesn't get recorded and what doesn't get counted — not who is lighting matches.
  • Every "unknown cause" finding is a data-quality finding, not evidence of a cover-up. An unrecorded cause could be anything from a genuinely undetermined origin to a resourcing gap in the office that fills out the form.

Chapter 1

The Monitoring Gap

The core finding

7.1%→38.2% BC's share of wildfires with no recorded cause, one year to the next — 1998 to 1999. It stayed elevated for two decades.

British Columbia keeps unusually good wildfire records — every fire since 1950, tagged Person, Lightning, or Unknown. That data shows a sudden, sustained collapse in cause-recording, not an arson wave: the unknown-cause rate was stable at 4-14% for decades, then jumped in a single year and didn't fully recover until the 2020s. Meanwhile, federal agencies responsible for wildfire coordination, satellite mapping, and weather monitoring saw staffing and budget cuts in 2026 — during one of the most intense fire seasons in Canadian history.

Chapter 2

It's Not Just BC

The core finding

100% of Manitoba's wildfires were coded "Unknown" cause for four straight seasons, 2000–2003 — then it reverted to normal recording.

BC isn't even the province with the most fire — the Northwest Territories and Saskatchewan each burn more land in a typical 20-year window. Using the Canadian National Fire Database (the one dataset with consistent per-fire cause coding nationwide), the same question BC's chapter asked turns up something starker in Manitoba, and an almost identical gap happening right now in Yukon — unresolved as of the most recent complete season.

Share of Canada's area burned by province/territory, 2005-2024 (Canadian National Fire Database).

Yukon's unknown-cause rate hit 97.6% in 2022 and was still at 29.5% in 2025 — unlike BC's, this gap hasn't closed.

Chapter 3

The Carbon Nobody Counts

The core finding

3.4× 2023's wildfire CO2 (peer-reviewed estimate) vs. Canada's entire official annual emissions total — and none of it is counted.

Canada's official 2023 greenhouse gas total was 694 megatonnes of CO2 — the number measured against Canada's climate targets. It contains zero tonnes of wildfire CO2: Canada reports wildfire emissions separately under a UN natural-disturbance provision and excludes them from the national count entirely. Independent estimates put 2023's wildfire season alone at 1.76 to 3 billion tonnes. This chapter also runs two honestly-labeled scenarios: cutting arson-style human ignition 90% barely moves the number, because lightning drives the overwhelming majority of area burned — but a cause-agnostic 90% cut via monitoring and rapid response could offset more CO2 than double Canada's entire oil & gas sector.

2023 wildfire CO2 estimates vs. Canada's official national total (694 Mt) and oil & gas sector (208 Mt).

Because wildfire CO2 isn't counted toward Canada's targets, this is a magnitude comparison for public accountability — not a literal accounting swap. That gap is itself part of the story.
