# Sharon Polsky / PACC -- Strengths & Weaknesses Analysis

*Analysis of her policy positions, recommendations, and approach as they relate to Canadian privacy enforcement reform*

---

## STRENGTHS

### S1. Correctly Identifies the Enforcement Gap as the Core Problem
The tweet thread proves her point: Loblaw fakes data deletion, Telus loses 1PB, CIRO exposes 750K SINs, PowerSchool exposes 2.77M children's records. Total fines: $0. Polsky has been saying this for years in committee. The evidence now overwhelmingly supports her diagnosis.

### S2. Deeply Credible and Institutionally Embedded
30+ years in privacy, PACC president, Privacy by Design Ambassador, CIO Strategy Council vice-chair, CIGI contributor, testified to ETHI, INDU, SECU, TRAN, and Senate committees. She is not an outsider yelling at clouds -- she's inside the machine and has been for decades. MPs on both sides of the aisle cite her approvingly in committee.

### S3. The Tribunal Critique Is Backed by a Former Privacy Commissioner
The 7-10 year delay estimate for the Tribunal path came from a previous OPC holder. This isn't speculation -- it's institutional knowledge. No other country has a privacy tribunal model. Her proposal (direct OPC order-making with Federal Court appeal) aligns with every major peer jurisdiction (EU, UK, Australia).

### S4. Represents the Practitioner Class, Not Industry or Government
PACC is independently funded -- not by government grants, not by Big Tech donations. She speaks for the people who actually implement privacy programs in organizations. This gives her credibility that industry lobbyists and government officials lack. When she says the consent model is broken, it's because her members see it fail daily.

### S5. Concrete, Actionable Recommendations
She doesn't just criticize. Her proposals are specific: reverse onus on corporations, public compliance index, mandatory PIA before surveillance deployment, certified privacy officers, executive personal liability. These are implementable legislative changes, not abstract principles.

### S6. Cross-Committee Presence Shows Breadth
She appeared before ETHI (privacy), INDU (industry/C-27), SECU (national security), and TRAN (transport) -- demonstrating that privacy intersects with every policy domain. She connects facial recognition, spyware, AI, data brokers, children's data, and cybersecurity into a coherent framework rather than treating them as siloed issues.

### S7. Her "Don't Enact C-27" Call Was Vindicated
Bill C-27 died without passage. The concerns she raised -- that it would make things worse by creating false confidence in a broken enforcement model -- were never rebutted. The current vacuum (no replacement tabled as of March 2029) is bad, but she would argue it's better than enshrining a system that delays enforcement by a decade.

---

## WEAKNESSES

### W1. "Don't Enact It" Without a Ready-Made Replacement
Polsky's primary recommendation was to kill C-27 entirely. While her criticisms were valid, this created a policy vacuum. Bill C-27 died, and nothing replaced it. In the meantime, every breach described in the tweet thread happened under PIPEDA -- the very regime she also criticizes. Her position risks becoming "the perfect is the enemy of the good." Some enforcement mechanism (even a flawed one with fines) would have been better than zero fines.

### W2. Witness Testimony Isn't Legislation
Despite 10+ committee appearances and published briefs, none of her recommendations were adopted into amendments. She has influence but not power. PACC has no lobbying registration, no PAC, and no direct legislative drafting role. Her credibility is high, but her conversion rate from recommendation to law is zero on the issues that matter most.

### W3. The Public Compliance Index Concept Is Untested
Her signature proposal -- a public compliance index where organizations demonstrate privacy adherence -- has no real-world precedent at scale. It could devolve into a checkbox exercise (like SOC 2 compliance) or be gamed by well-resourced companies. She hasn't published a detailed implementation framework for how this would work in practice.

### W4. Individual Executive Liability Is Politically Difficult
While morally compelling, personal fines for executives face enormous political resistance. Corporate lobbies (ITAC, Chamber of Commerce, BCC) would fight this aggressively. She hasn't built a coalition or political strategy to overcome this resistance -- she's making the argument in committee but not doing the political groundwork to make it legislatively viable.

### W5. Limited Digital Presence and Public Mobilization
Her Twitter/X account (@PolskySays) has limited reach. PACC's media presence is mostly reactive (responding to breaches and bills). She doesn't publish regular policy analysis, doesn't maintain a Substack or newsletter, and doesn't build public pressure campaigns. Compare this to OpenMedia or the CCLA, which have larger public followings and can mobilize citizen support. Her influence is elite-level (committee rooms) but not grassroots.

### W6. Doesn't Engage With the "Move Fast" Tech Policy Reality
Her recommendations assume a deliberate, thorough legislative process. But the reality is: C-27 took 3+ years and died. A new bill would take years more. Meanwhile, AI capabilities advance monthly. Her framework doesn't account for how to provide interim enforcement while waiting for ideal legislation. She needs a "what do we do RIGHT NOW" answer beyond "not C-27."

### W7. The GDPR Comparison Has Limits
She frequently points to the EU's GDPR as a model. But GDPR enforcement itself has been criticized for slow cross-border processes, with Ireland's DPC (where most Big Tech is headquartered) routinely delaying actions for years. The direct enforcement model she advocates has its own documented failures in the jurisdiction she holds up as exemplary. A more nuanced comparison would strengthen her argument.

### W8. Resistance to Sharing Publicly
Per the original context: *"I'm not about to tell all my ideas to an anonymous account, even one with a blue tick mark."* This is understandable for protecting intellectual property, but it limits her ability to build the public coalition needed to actually change the law. The most effective privacy advocates (Michael Geist, Jim Balsillie, Ann Cavoukian) are prolific public communicators. Her reticence to share works against her policy goals.

---

## Net Assessment

Polsky is **the right diagnostician** -- her analysis of what's broken in Canadian privacy enforcement is precise, evidence-backed, and now empirically validated by a string of zero-consequence breaches. Her weakness is on the **implementation and political strategy side**: she knows what the law should say but hasn't built the political machinery to make it happen. For anyone building a tool to hold organizations accountable (like the tweet author suggests), her committee testimony and PACC briefs are the single best source of *what* needs to change. The gap is *how* to make it happen outside of committee rooms.
