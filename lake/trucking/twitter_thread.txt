Canada may not be able to import new U.S. heavy-duty trucks in 2027.

Not because of tariffs. Not a trade dispute. Not an emissions disagreement.

Because a regulatory citation hasn't been updated.

🧵

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
INTRO (no screenshot)
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Since 2018, Canada's import regulations for heavy-duty trucks have required U.S. EPA certification.

In February 2026, the EPA formally eliminated its authority to issue those certifications. NHTSA is now the only federal certifier — and Canada's regulations don't reference NHTSA.

~$5B in annual truck purchases. The ordering window is August 2026. ECCC's own regulatory documents acknowledged in April 2024 that amendments would be needed.

Here's what happened.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
PART 1: THE GAP
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[screenshot: 01-gap]
1/
Canada's import regulations require EPA certification for heavy-duty trucks. The U.S. EPA no longer issues those certifications — NHTSA does.

The trucks haven't changed. Safety and emissions standards remain aligned between the two countries. The gap is a single citation in a regulation that hasn't been updated to reflect the change in certifying body.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
PART 2: THE DEPENDENCY
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[screenshot: 02-dependency]
2/
Canada sources 95% of its Class 8 heavy-duty trucks from U.S. manufacturers — Kenworth, Peterbilt, Freightliner, International, Mack.

These are not off-the-lot purchases. Class 8 trucks are built-to-order, 12–18 months in advance.

Missing the ordering window means missing the supply for that model year. There is no substitute.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
PART 3: HOW THE GAP WAS CREATED
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[screenshot: 03-timeline]
3/
May 2018: Canada harmonizes its heavy-duty GHG regulations with U.S. EPA Phase 2 standards.

The key provision: "any engine certified by the EPA may be sold in Canada."

This worked because Phase 1 (2011) and Phase 2 (2016) were joint EPA and NHTSA rules — both agencies co-certified.

4/
April 2024: The U.S. EPA finalizes Phase 3 GHG standards for 2027+ model trucks — as an EPA-only rule.

NHTSA coordinated but did not co-finalize.

Canada's own Environment department noted in its regulatory filings: "amendments to the HD GHG Emission Regulations will be needed."

No proposed amendments were published.

5/
February 12, 2026: The U.S. EPA formally rescinds the 2009 Greenhouse Gas Endangerment Finding — the legal foundation for all GHG vehicle certifications.

EPA can no longer legally issue emissions certificates for heavy-duty trucks.

NHTSA becomes the only remaining federal certifier. Canada's regulations still require EPA.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
PART 4: THE FINANCIAL SCALE
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[screenshot: 06-financial]
6/
What's at stake in direct truck purchases:

→ ~17,000–20,000 Class 8 trucks/year in Canada
→ Average price: ~$265,000 CAD
→ Annual market: $4.5–5.3B CAD

Essentially 100% of that volume is built-to-order.
No order placed = no truck for the 2027 model year. No workaround.

7/
That direct figure is the first layer. The chain continues:

→ ~$595M/yr in incremental fleet operating costs on un-renewed equipment
→ Estimated $3–6B in freight rate pressure passed to shippers (order-of-magnitude; 5–10% capacity squeeze on a $65B industry)
→ Construction, mining, forestry, and agriculture absorb higher input costs

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
PART 5: FINDINGS
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[screenshot: 08-findings]
8/
FINDING: Canada's 2018 harmonization was not a treaty or bilateral agreement.

It was a unilateral domestic regulation under CEPA, motivated by a non-binding 2016 Obama-Trudeau Joint Statement.

That statement had no ratification, no enforcement mechanism, and does not bind the current U.S. administration.

The U.S. changed its own domestic rules.

9/
FINDING: ECCC's own regulatory documents acknowledged the gap in April 2024.

Following the Phase 3 rule, ECCC explicitly noted: "amendments to the HD GHG Emission Regulations will be needed in Canada to ensure continued alignment."

April 2024 to August 2026 is over two years. No proposed amendments were published in the Canada Gazette.

10/
FINDING: The trailer regulations provide relevant precedent.

When U.S. trailer GHG standards were vacated in 2019, Canada did not amend its regulations. It issued a ministerial interim order under CEPA section 163(3) — and has renewed it seven times since.

A ministerial interim order is the fastest available mechanism here. It expires after 12 months and does not constitute a regulatory amendment.

──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
PART 6: WHAT SHOULD HAPPEN
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[screenshot: 11-recommendations]
11/
When UK Brexit left vehicle type approval regulations citing a body that no longer had authority, targeted statutory instruments (SI 2019/648) updated the certifying body references within 12 months.

Transport Canada has the same mechanism. Amend the Motor Vehicle Safety Act regulations to recognize NHTSA before August 2026. A ministerial interim order is the fastest first step; a full amendment is the durable fix.

12/
Transport Canada's own 2026 MVSA consultation explicitly debated making foreign standard references "ambulatory" — automatically tracking changes rather than fixed to specific versions.

The current gap is a direct consequence of a fixed EPA citation. Ambulatory references would prevent the next one structurally, without emergency legislation.
