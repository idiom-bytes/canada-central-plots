The Bloomberg headline says "Canada draws the most foreign investment since 2007."

That's technically true.

But which sectors got the money — and is any of it actually building Canada?

Here's the full breakdown, the findings, and what we should do about it. 🧵

───────────────────────────────────────
PART 1: WHERE THE MONEY WENT (BY SECTOR)
───────────────────────────────────────

1/ THE SIX BUCKETS

Statistics Canada breaks FDI inflows into six NAICS industry categories.

2025 totals (C$ billions):

  Manufacturing:              $11.2B  (12%)
  Trade & Transportation:     $23.6B  (25%)
  Energy & Mining:             $9.6B  (10%)
  Finance & Insurance:        $11.1B  (12%)
  Management of Companies:    $14.5B  (16%)
  Other Industries:           $23.5B  (25%)

  TOTAL:                      $93.6B

───────────────────────────────────────

2/ MANUFACTURING IS THE STORY — BUT NOT HOW YOU THINK

Manufacturing is the sector you want dominant. Factories. Jobs. Industrial capacity.

In 2025, it was $11.2B — 12% of total inflows. Below average.

In 2024, it spiked to $43.1B. A record. Nearly triple any other year on record.

That was companies front-running US tariffs — racing to establish Canadian manufacturing before the trade wall went up. It evaporated the moment the window closed.

───────────────────────────────────────

3/ THE QUIET SECTOR NOBODY COVERS: MANAGEMENT OF COMPANIES

"Management of Companies and Enterprises" is the line that never gets a headline.

In plain terms: a foreign holding company acquires a Canadian business. No new factory. No new jobs from scratch. Ownership transferred abroad.

  2014: $15.2B
  2015: $14.0B
  2022: $14.4B
  2025: $14.5B

$14–15B in peak years, running consistently. The profit streams of acquired Canadian businesses now flow to foreign balance sheets. That's not investment in Canada. That's divestment of Canada.

───────────────────────────────────────

4/ ENERGY & MINING: THE VOLATILE ONE

Energy & Mining twice went negative — net divestment:

  2017: −$4.9B  (more capital left than entered)
  2020: −$9.3B  (COVID-driven capital flight from Canadian oil & gas)

When it's positive: foreign entities acquiring Canadian resource assets.
When it goes negative: they're pulling out.

Neither direction is "Canada wins."

───────────────────────────────────────
PART 2: THE GREEN / RED BREAKDOWN
───────────────────────────────────────

5/ THE KISS TEST

Not all FDI is the same. A simple two-bucket split:

GREEN — productive investment:
  • Manufacturing: builds factories, jobs, industrial capacity
  • Trade & Transportation: logistics and distribution infrastructure

RED — extractive / control-seeking capital:
  • Energy & Mining: foreign ownership of Canadian resources
  • Finance & Insurance: financial flows, often repatriated
  • Management of Companies: foreign acquisition of existing Canadian businesses
  • Other: residual bucket including real estate

───────────────────────────────────────

6/ 2025 BY THE SPLIT

  GREEN:  $34.8B  (37%)
  RED:    $58.8B  (63%)

63 cents of every dollar flowing into Canada in 2025 is not building new Canadian capacity.

It is acquiring control of existing assets, extracting resources, or moving through financial structures.

───────────────────────────────────────
PART 3: FINDINGS
───────────────────────────────────────

8/ FINDING #1: THE RECORD NUMBER IS REAL. THE COMPOSITION ISN'T WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE.

$93.6B is the highest since 2007.

But the productive share — manufacturing plus trade infrastructure — was just $34.8B. 37%.

The headline reflects investor appetite for Canadian assets. It does not reflect investors betting on Canada's industrial future.

───────────────────────────────────────

9/ FINDING #2: 2024'S "GOOD" YEAR WAS A TARIFF SUGAR HIGH

When a single sector triples in one year and collapses the next, that's not a structural shift.

Companies rushed to establish Canadian manufacturing ahead of US tariffs. The window closed. The capital stopped.

The underlying composition of Canadian FDI didn't change. We got a one-time arbitrage play dressed up as a record year.

───────────────────────────────────────

10/ FINDING #3: MANAGEMENT OF COMPANIES IS THE REAL STORY NOBODY IS COVERING

$14–15B per year in foreign holding companies acquiring Canadian businesses.

No new capacity. No new employment. Just Canadian businesses — and their future profit streams — transferred to foreign balance sheets. Consistently. Every year. No coverage.

───────────────────────────────────────

11/ FINDING #4: THE DEFAULT STATE OF CANADIAN FDI IS EXTRACTION

Red > Green in 14 of 19 years.

The exceptions are years with a manufacturing spike (usually tariff-driven) or a collapse in red-category inflows (COVID, energy crashes). Remove those anomalies and the base case is clear:

The majority of foreign capital entering Canada is acquiring control, not building capacity.

───────────────────────────────────────
PART 4: WHAT SHOULD BE DONE ABOUT IT
───────────────────────────────────────

12/ RECOMMENDATION 1: TIGHTEN INVESTMENT CANADA ACT REVIEWS FOR COMPANY ACQUISITIONS

The current net benefit review threshold is $1.075B for WTO investors. The vast majority of foreign acquisitions of Canadian businesses pass unscrutinized.

A sector-specific lower threshold — or a mandatory public-interest test for all Management of Companies FDI above $100M — would close this gap.

Foreign holding companies shouldn't be able to buy Canadian businesses at scale with no public-interest review.

───────────────────────────────────────

13/ RECOMMENDATION 2: CONDITION RESOURCE FDI ON DOMESTIC VALUE-ADD

Foreign entities own our oil, gas, and minerals. The profits repatriate abroad.

New resource FDI approvals should carry binding conditions: minimum Canadian processing ratios, mandatory reinvestment thresholds, or royalty structures that increase as value-add declines.

Norway built a sovereign wealth fund from this logic. Canada is still giving the resource away.

───────────────────────────────────────

14/ RECOMMENDATION 3: LOCK IN THE 2024 MANUFACTURING PLANTS BEFORE THEY LEAVE

Companies built Canadian manufacturing capacity in 2024 to dodge US tariffs. That window is closing.

Budget 2026 should deploy targeted retention instruments — multi-year operating cost supports, workforce co-investments, anchor-tenant supply chain incentives — to keep facilities that were established opportunistically before they redomicile back south.

We got the plants. Now we have to keep them.

───────────────────────────────────────

15/ RECOMMENDATION 4: REQUIRE THE GOVERNMENT TO PUBLISH THE PRODUCTIVE-SHARE RATIO

Every Budget and Fall Economic Statement announces total FDI inflows as a win. None of them report what sectors it went into.

Parliament should direct the Minister of Finance to publish the annual green/red sector breakdown in every FDI communication.

A headline number without composition is not transparency. It is marketing.

───────────────────────────────────────

16/ RECOMMENDATION 5: ESTABLISH A PARLIAMENTARY FDI COMPOSITION KPI

The productive share has averaged ~39% over 2007–2025 with no upward trend.

Parliament has no formal mechanism to hold the government accountable for this ratio.

A Finance Committee motion to establish an annual FDI Composition Report — modelled on the Fiscal Monitor — would create the accountability infrastructure that currently does not exist.

Track it. Report it. Answer for it.

───────────────────────────────────────

17/ RECOMMENDATION 6: REBUILD FIPAS AROUND CONTRIBUTION, NOT JUST PROTECTION

Canada's Foreign Investment Promotion and Protection Agreements protect investor rights in Canada.

None of them set minimum thresholds for what investors must contribute.

Future FIPAs — and renegotiation of existing ones — should include binding commitments: employment creation minimums, R&D spending ratios, technology transfer obligations. Investor protection in exchange for investor contribution.
